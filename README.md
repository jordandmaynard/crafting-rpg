# Emberwild

A fantasy survival game in the spirit of V Rising, with the same low-res, isometric pixel look as [Dungeon Golf](https://github.com/jordandmaynard/dungeon-golf) and [Bone Road](https://github.com/jordandmaynard/board-game-rpg).

You are a human adventurer in a wild land that is generated fresh every time: 1024 × 1024 tiles of terraced ground on a strict grid, with half-tile slopes and ramps between the levels. Fell trees and break rocks for wood and stone, fight what comes out of the wilds, and build a shelter before night falls.

## Play

Open `index.html` in a browser. It is a single file; Three.js r128 loads from cdnjs. Generating the world takes a couple of seconds.

Every load rolls a new world. To replay one, pin its seed (shown on the title screen and the map): `index.html?seed=12345` or `index.html#seed-12345`.

## Controls

- **Move:** WASD (W always walks up the screen). You always face the cursor as you walk, so you can back away from something while facing it. The forward walk, a backward walk and a sidestep blend smoothly by which way you step against the way you face.
- **Attack:** left click, aimed at the cursor. Hold to keep swinging; the third swing of a combo is a heavy overhead. Hitting trees and rocks gathers wood and stone.
- **Q** whirlwind, **E** lunge, **Space** dash. The whirlwind and the lunge also cut and break trees and rocks they reach, and every blow gathers wood or stone in proportion to its damage.
- **F** open and close doors.
- **Camera:** hold the right mouse button and drag to turn it freely (the middle button works too). The tilt always stays at 30°. **Z** / **C** turn it a quarter at a time, and the mouse wheel zooms.
- **B** build mode: **1** floors (wood, stone), **2** walls (timber, log palisade, stone), **3** doors, **4** hearth. Each number opens its own row of kinds over the bar, and the numbers then pick from that row. Left click places; drag to lay a rectangle of floor, or a straight wall along whichever way you drag (as many pieces as you can pay for). A right click (without dragging) removes a piece for a full refund. Every button on the bar can be clicked too.
- **M** map: the mouse wheel zooms it (and the minimap) and dragging pans it. The minimap slides and turns every frame and repaints its contents a few times a second.
- **T** tuning panel: the tile height per level (1, the original height, or 0.5; letting go of the slider rebuilds the same world from scratch, so you lose what you've built), the camera (orthographic, or perspective with a field of view you set), the mosaic pixel size and anti-aliasing (the scene is drawn finer and averaged into each mosaic pixel, so thin lines blend instead of breaking up), the hero's light (strength by night and day, reach, colour, and how far toward the camera and how high it hangs, so the side you see stays lit), the day and night (time, clock speed, latitude, season, sun, sky and moon light, haze, exposure), sun and moon shadows (on or off, strength, reach round the view, softness), distance haze (where it starts and thickens, how thick, and its colour by day, dusk and night), the walk (cadence, forward and backward stride, arm swing, how wide a sidestep opens, bob, lean), cloud shadows (strength, cover, softness, size, drift speed and direction), the water (clarity, body brightness, wave height and speed, river flow, sun glitter, foam), the black edge outline, and a second Sobel crease outline (its own colour, strength, angle and thickness) (how far back the ground behind an edge must be, and how many mosaic pixels thick).

### Xbox controller

Press any button on a controller and the game switches to it: the action bar, the door prompt, the help and the map show Xbox button glyphs, and a key or the mouse switches back.

- **Play:** left stick moves, right stick aims (let go and you face the way you walk), **RT** attacks (hold to keep swinging), **X** whirlwind, **Y** lunge, **A** dash, **B** opens and closes doors.
- **Build (MENU):** the right stick moves a diamond cursor around you. **RT** places (hold it and move the cursor to lay a row or an area), **LT** removes, **X** / **Y** / **A** open the floor, wall and door rows (press again to step to the next kind), **RB** hearth, **B** closes the row or leaves build mode.
- **Both:** **VIEW** map (left stick pans, **RT** / **LT** zoom, **B** closes), **L3** plot outlines, D-pad up and down zoom, left and right turn the camera.

## The world

- **Terrain:** eight height levels over open water. Each level is one tile high by default (half a tile if you set it in the tuning panel). The lowlands are broad, gentle country. Most of the steep ground is gathered into a few mountain ranges. Terraces are at least three tiles deep (no ground stands more than a level over anything within two tiles of it), so steep ground comes out as a few broad terraces, not a staircase, and the side of every terrace is a slope that reaches half a tile into the lower ground, so you can't walk up it. Rocks of the local kind (mossy stone, sandstone ledges, snow-capped boulders) are strewn down every slope and along its top and foot. The meadows roll gently and climb mostly by ramps, while the snowy heights are steeper, with sheer terraces and few ramps. Ramps, two tiles long wherever there's room, join the terraces. After the ramps are carved, a flood fill from the first town adds more ramps until every stretch of land you could walk to is reachable.
- **Lakes, rivers and canyons:** about two dozen lakes fill hollows in the high ground, each to just under the lowest bank round it, with a sandy shelf round the edge that shelves away into hollows and deeps of varying depth, and each spills over into a river. More rivers rise on the heights. They follow the drainage of a priority flood in from the sea, so they only run level or downhill, lying level across their width a level under their lowest bank, and wider as they go, and end in the sea, a lake or another river; where one drops a level its water falls in white rapids. Seven deep canyons wind through the high ground, most of them across the way between two towns. Nothing can cross water or a canyon except over a bridge.
- **Biomes:** Bone Road's four (the Whispering Wood, Mirefen Bog, the Hollow Graves, and towns like Emberbrook), plus Goldenreach Meadows, the Sunscorch Waste desert, and the Frostspine snow on the high ground.
- **The Elderwood (deep forest):** the heart of any wood big enough, about ten tiles in from its edge, so it's always ringed by ordinary forest. Its floor is brown-green with fallen needles. Redwoods stand on a loose grid, one to a 4 × 4 block at most. They can't be felled, and nobody can claim plots there. Their trunks run off the top of the view; the trunks between the camera and the bottom of the view are left out, thinning away in a dither, so they don't wall off the screen. The crowns are up out of sight: they draw nothing but cast the sun's and moon's shadows, so the floor is dappled. Under them grow ferns, toadstools, logs and the odd young pine. Giant spiders run in twos and threes. Mantraps are rooted: they turn to face you, and once you come within a few tiles they rear back and lunge their heads out to bite.
- **Roads:** roads join the towns and three waystones in every biome. They only change level by ramps, cross rivers and canyons on plank bridges (piled into the river bed, or hung from rope rails over a canyon), a street only runs out to the edge of town where it carries on as a road (one that can't get out, past cliffs or water, stops at the last door on it), and every road ends at a town or a waystone.
- **Towns:** seven per world, each with a plaza, a well, streets and four houses built from the same walls, doors, floors and roofs you build with. Monsters keep out of town, and you wake by the well in Emberbrook if you fall.
- **Foes:** goblins and wolves in the wood, slimes and zombies in the bog, ghosts, zombies and skeletons in the graves, boars and goblins in the meadows, frost wolves and ice wraiths in the snow, scorpions and skeletons in the desert. More come out at night, and they hunt farther.

## Building

The wilds outside town are marked out into plots, as in V Rising: about 450 a world, each some 35 to 50 tiles across. Each one lies on a single terrace and stops at cliffs, water, roads and towns, so nearly every plot is flat ground to build on. A plot may also take in a knoll or dip beside it that is too small to be a plot of its own. Steep or broken ground that no plot fits stays unclaimable. Press **P** (or the map's PLOTS button) to show their outlines on the map and minimap, white until claimed; hovering over one there, or on the ground in build mode, washes the whole plot white. Nothing can be built on a plot until you claim it with a hearth (4 stone, 2 wood): a round stone firepit under a hood, with a chimney that rises clear of any roof built over it. You can lay a floor under it. Build a wall (not a door) on one side of it and it becomes a fireplace built into that wall, with jambs, a mantel and a chimney breast. It stays on that wall while the wall stands. If that wall comes down, it moves to another wall only when just one is left round it, and it turns back into the round pit once there are no walls round it. A claimed plot turns blue. You can have one hearth. Standing near it heals you, you wake beside it when you fall, and taking it down gives the plot up.

Floors need flat ground that no slope reaches into. Walls (not doors) can also run along the top of a one-level cliff: only the ground on top has to be clear and flat, the rocks along that edge make way, and the wall stands on the high side, overhanging the slope a little. Wood floors cost 2 wood, stone floors 2 stone, timber walls 2 wood and 2 stone, log palisade walls 4 wood, stone walls 4 stone, doors 5 wood. Close in a room with walls and doors (any shape, up to about 500 tiles) and a hipped roof goes up over it. Rooms that meet across a wall or door, like a room walled off inside a bigger one, make one building under one roof. The roof hides while you stand anywhere in the building, and every roof hides in build mode. Inside, every wall of the building you're in (your room and any rooms joined to it) that is both lower on the screen than you and between you and the camera, and every outer wall whose inside faces away from the camera, drops to waist height so you can see in. Doors always stand full height. You heal faster under a roof, and monsters can't get through walls or closed doors.

## How it renders

The Dungeon Golf pipeline: a low-resolution WebGL buffer scaled up by a whole number, an orthographic camera at a fixed 30° tilt (starting at a 45° turn, where tile edges are a clean 2:1 pixel staircase), and a camera snapped to whole big pixels with the leftover slid in CSS. With the outline on, the frame renders to a target with a depth texture, and a pass on the low-res buffer strokes any pixel whose neighbours within the outline's thickness lie more than the outline distance further back, as Crafting and Conquest's toon strokes do, so strokes are whole mosaic pixels. Scenery is merged into vertex-coloured, flat-shaded meshes, and the hero draws in a second pass so a silhouette shows through anything in front.

The 1024 × 1024 map is split into 32 × 32-tile chunks. Only chunks near the camera are built, nearest first and a few milliseconds' worth per frame, and far ones are let go. Felling a tree or building a wall rebuilds only its chunk.

## Water

Crafting and Conquest's water surface, brought down to WebGL. Fresnel blends the sky's reflection with the water body (light scattered back up out of it), sun glitter comes off the wave slopes, and whatever lies under the surface shows through the water above it, each colour absorbed at its own rate (red first). A lace of foam runs along the shore, and the water washes it in and out. The waves are scrolling slope noise instead of an FFT ocean. Rivers carry the same water along their flow, using a flow map with two phases half a cycle apart so the stretch never builds up, streaked with foam. The surface is drawn in two passes over what's below: one multiplies it by what the water lets through, then the next adds the water's own light. The sea uses it too, and shelves off from the coast.

## Day, night and clouds

The day and night follow Crafting and Conquest's sky without drawing it: the sun is placed by the hour, latitude, season and north; its light reddens and dims through the air at a low sun (Rayleigh and haze extinction over the air mass); the sky's light shades from night through dusk to day; the moon, opposite the sun, lights the night; and the exposure opens up as the light fails. Cloud shadows are a tiling noise laid over the world and slowly scrolled, dimming only the sun's or moon's light in every lit material's shader, so lamps and the torch are untouched.
