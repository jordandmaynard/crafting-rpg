# Emberwild

A fantasy survival game in the spirit of V Rising, with the same low-res, isometric pixel look as [Dungeon Golf](https://github.com/jordandmaynard/dungeon-golf) and [Bone Road](https://github.com/jordandmaynard/board-game-rpg).

You are a human adventurer in a wild land that is generated fresh every time: 1216 × 1216 tiles of terraced ground on a strict grid, with half-tile slopes and ramps between the levels. Fell trees and break rocks for wood and stone, fight what comes out of the wilds, and build a shelter before night falls.

## Play

Serve the folder and open `index.html` in a browser with WebGPU (a current Chrome, Edge or Safari, or Firefox with WebGPU on). The game is in `index.html`; it draws through `src/gfx/`, a small WebGPU library of its own, so it has to be served over http(s) (a `file://` page can't load the modules, and WebGPU needs a secure origin; `localhost` counts). `npx serve` or `python3 -m http.server` in the folder will do. Generating the world takes a couple of seconds.

Every load rolls a new world unless you keep one. The map has a seed box (type a seed and LOAD SEED, or Enter) and an EACH LOAD button that switches between a new seed every load and keeping this one (remembered in the browser; NEW WORLD rolls a fresh one, kept from then on if you're keeping). A seed in the address still wins: `index.html?seed=12345`, or `index.html#seed-12345` for one load.

## Controls

- **Move:** WASD (W always walks up the screen). You always face the cursor as you walk, so you can back away from something while facing it. The forward walk, a backward walk and a sidestep blend smoothly by which way you step against the way you face.
- **Joints:** the hero bends at the knees, ankles, elbows, wrists and waist (each limb's box cut in two where the joint is, so the blocky look is the same standing still). Walking, each knee bends as its leg swings through, the feet stay near level with the ground, the arms bend at the elbow as they swing, and the shoulders turn against the hips; the tumble and the flip fold the knees. T > Walk has each one (knee, foot, elbow, twist, tuck): set them all to 0 for the old stiff-limbed walk. The sword's blows use them (T > Sword moves, each part 0 to take it out): each sweep winds up first (arm drawn back, elbow cocked, wrist laid back, shoulders turned away) and then cuts through with the shoulders turning after it, the wrist snapping over and the knees dipping, the free arm thrown out the other way; the third blow is raised high behind the head and brought down with a step onto the left foot and a lean. Cleave and the axe's buzzsaw spin crouched with both arms out wide, and the lunge drives into a deep front knee with the back leg straight and the free arm flung back. The bow uses them too (T > Bow moves): the shoulders turn on round past the hips so you stand side-on, the feet set apart and the knees soft; the bow arm reaches out ahead and the wrist turns the bow back onto the aim, so the arrow and string lie out in front of the chest; the drawing hand takes the string there and draws it back, the elbow bending and rising out behind along the arrow; on the loose the hand flies on back past the ear, the bow arm dips and the string shivers. The volley lays the bow over flat and crouches, and the rain leans back with the weight on the back foot. The spear's, axe's and hammer's attacks don't use the joints yet (the hammer's two sweeps share the sword's).
- **Attack:** left click, aimed at the cursor. Hold to keep swinging; the third swing of a combo is a heavy overhead. Hitting trees and rocks gathers wood and stone.
- **Q** cleave (v323; it was the whirlwind): one quick sweep all round that strikes every foe, tree and rock within 1.8 tiles once and leaves foes bleeding for 3 damage every half second for 4 seconds (8 s cooldown; T > Cleave), **E** lunge, **Space** dash: a forward tumble that you steer with the movement keys, untouchable the whole way, and you come up out of it with a burst of extra run speed for a moment. **Shift** (A on a controller) jumps off a cliff: when you push against the top of a drop with flat ground at its foot, SHIFT TO JUMP shows over your head, and you front-flip down onto that ground. It has no cooldown. Cleave and the lunge also cut and break trees and rocks they reach, and every blow gathers wood or stone in proportion to its damage.
- **Tab** opens the inventory (since v326 the Wanderer's Kit character folio and backpack, side by side; drag them by either title, close with either X or Tab): six gear slots round a live view of the hero (helmet, torso, amulet, weapon, pants, boots; nothing fills the amulet yet) and a 4×5 backpack, one item to a square. Click (or right-click) an item to put it on or take it off, or drag it to a square. You start in a cloth shirt, cloth pants and cloth boots with a basic sword. Your weapon gives your attack and your **Q** and **E** abilities: take the sword off and you punch, with nothing on Q or E. Take off the shirt and pants and you're down to heart boxer shorts.
- **The spear:** a wooden spear starts in your pack too. With it on, your attack is a quick poke straight out the way you aim, striking every foe along a short line (2.2 tiles), or with none in the way chopping the tree or breaking the rock in front, as a sword stroke does (the flurry harvests too, the harpoon doesn't). While attacking the hero holds it low and drawn back, arm swung back by the hip (its tip 0.6 tiles ahead), and drives it out hard (to 1.25 tiles ahead) without the hand ever leaving the shaft: the reach comes from leaning in at the waist, turning the shoulder into it and swinging the arm up level. All of that is in T > Spear. **Q** is a flurry: 2 seconds of rapid jabs into a cone in front, set the way you aimed when it began; you can walk (slowly) but it doesn't turn (8 s cooldown). Each jab strikes the whole cone; the hero's spear goes in at a different spot each time and pale ghost spears dart out at others between them, too fast to see (count, life and glow in T > Spear). **E** throws the spear itself out on a chain (9 tiles): the first foe it meets is struck and reeled back to you (dragged round walls, not through them), then slowed by 90% for 2 seconds (7 s cooldown). Both show their reach on the ground and fire on release with the Attack on release option. T > Spear has the numbers; T > Aim decal the flurry cone's and harpoon box's look. **Both hands (v316, T > Spear moves):** while attacking, the right hand holds it near the butt by the hip and the left grips the shaft ahead, the shaft laid across the body toward a tip out in front of the middle. The shoulders turn round to the right so the left arm can reach (the head turned back onto the aim), the left foot steps ahead, and a poke drives the right hand through with a small step in, the shaft sliding through the left hand until the hands meet. Both arms are bent onto their grips each frame (two-bone reach); Spear moves > Both hands 0 brings back the old one-handed hold. **The harpoon (E) is thrown like a javelin (v317):** hoisted up and back over the right shoulder, tipped up, the shoulders turned away, the left arm pointing at the mark and the weight on the back foot; it flies as the arm whips over and through (0.6 s in all, letting go at 55%; T > Spear moves, the Throw rows).
- **The bow:** you also start with a wooden bow in your pack. It's carried level in the hanging hand, string above the grip; to shoot the hero turns side-on (45° to the right, T > Bow), head and bow arm still on the aim, and draws the string back to the face across the body, holding the stance while the attack is held. With it on, your attack shoots an arrow dead straight (no arc) at chest height the way you aim, up to 20 tiles. It sticks in the first thing it meets: a foe of any size on your level (where it stays, riding along as the foe moves), a tree, rock, wall, fence, closed door or cliff. It never harvests or harms trees and rocks. **Q** looses a volley of 5 arrows over a 45° cone that knocks foes back a tile (5 s cooldown). **E** shoots an arrow straight up, and a second later 10 arrows rain down in a ring round where the cursor was (an orange ring marks the spot; 8 s cooldown; with a pad or touch it's 8 tiles along your aim). The rain seeks out foes: as each arrow starts down it takes a foe in the ring that none has yet, so each one there gets at least one while the arrows last, and steers onto it as it falls while it stays in the ring; once they all have one, half the rest (T > Bow: rainSeek) pick a foe too. Spent arrows stay stuck for 20 seconds. T > Bow has the damage, speeds, range, counts, cone, ring, knock-backs and cooldowns. While you hold the attack with the bow, a thin box glows on the ground out to its range (on water and deep snow and sand too). With **Attack on release** on in Options (O), holding Q or E shows the volley's cone or the rain's ring instead, and letting go shoots (with the sword, holding E shows the lunge's reach as a box the width it strikes, pointed at its far end, and letting go lunges; the whirl and the swing are point blank and still go on the press); the touch buttons always work that way. It's a decal: a sheet over the ground and water round the shape, drawn last, that finds the surface seen through each pixel from a copy of the depth and lights the shape there if it's near the ground. Each shape is still: a faint fill edged brighter along its sides (the shot a thin box, the volley its cone, the rain a disc). Arrows stir the ground haze: each one flying low draws a thin clear wake, pushing the haze out from its line and dragging the air along, and each one that comes down (the rain, or a shot dropping at the end of its range) clears a small spot and pushes the haze out round it, and pokes a small hole in deep snow or sand that fills back in like a trench (T > Deep snow and sand: arrow hole) (T > Ground haze: arrow wake and arrow landing; the sim takes up to 32 such stirs a frame in a small buffer). T > Aim decal has the colour and, for each shape, the edge width, edge brightness and fill brightness (and the shot box's width).
- Wolves (grey and frost) and boars leave a hide where they fall; walk up and press **F** (**A** on a controller) to pick it up. Hides stack to 100 in a pack square. Logs and stone go straight into the pack too, 200 to a square, and there's no separate wood and stone count any more; when the pack is full they fall on the ground as piles to pick up with F / A. One house in every town is the tannery, with an armor crafting table inside: use it (F / A) to make leather armor (5 leather), leather pants (4), leather boots (2) and a leather helmet (2), or the stronger sets of leather and their material: bone, copper chain (copper ingots) and iron plate (iron ingots), each an armor, leggings, boots and helmet for 3/2/1/1 leather and 6/4/3/3 of the material. Armor points (shown in the gear window; since v324 your armor takes armor ÷ (armor + 8 × the attacker's level) off each blow, at most 75%, so a full iron plate set of 14 stops 64% from a level 1 foe but 15% from a level 10; hover ARMOR for yours against a foe of your level. The level gap scales damage too: each level you're over a foe, your blows on it do 10% more (compounding) and its blows on you 10% less, and the reverse when it's over you, counting up to 10 levels. Foes' health grows with their level too, 35% of their base for each level over 1 (a wolf has 32 at level 1, 122 at 9, 133 at 10), so band 9-10 foes take about as many blows from an Auralith weapon as band 1 foes do from a wooden one (v325); T > Levels) are leather 3/2/1/1, bone 4/2/1/2, copper chain 4/3/2/2 and iron plate 5/4/2/3 (chest, legs, boots, head). The hero wears each set in its colours with pieces of its own: bone horns and ribs, a chain coif and skirt, an iron crest, nasal guard, breastplate and knee plates. A trash can under the pack throws away whatever is dragged onto it (a whole stack).
- **The axe and the hammer:** a wooden axe and a wooden hammer start in your pack too (hotbar 4 and 5), and like the other weapons they come in every material, each one grander than the last (wood is a bare whittled blade or mallet, bone is bound and toothed, copper has a leather grip and copper caps, iron is double-bitted or spiked, gold-mounted and set with a gem; the fantasy metals are the iron ones in the metal). The axe's attack is three chops, all cutting down on the diagonal (the last a big one from overhead). The hero carries it with the blade pointing down (and swings it blade first). **Q** throws it spinning flat out the way you aim, with a pale swoosh trailing the blade and a whoosh each turn: it slows to a stop 7 tiles out, always going the full way unless a cliff face or something built (a wall, fence, shut door, house, station or crate) stops it first: it chops through trees and rocks, rises up ramps and sails out over drops (v320) and then speeds back up to you like a boomerang, striking every foe, tree and rock it passes on the way out and again on the way back (6 s cooldown). **E** is the buzzsaw (v323; it was rend): a long spin of a second, striking every foe, tree and rock within 1.75 tiles four times a second (8 s cooldown). The hammer is carried with one face pointing down, and its head is big (twice the size it first was, the end caps standing just proud of its faces). Its attack is two heavy sweeps and then an uppercut that knocks foes well back. **Q** is a stun smash: the hammer slams the ground and every foe in a 70° cone 2.6 tiles deep is struck and stunned for 1.5 s, and trees and rocks in it are struck too (8 s cooldown). **E** is a charge: you run forward with the hammer out in front; foes, trees and rocks you meet are struck and foes carried along ahead of you, and if you or they run into a wall, cliff, tree or anything solid they are struck again, harder (9 s cooldown). Axes do double damage to trees and hammers double to rocks and ore. The throw, smash and charge show their reach on the ground. All numbers are in T > Axe and T > Hammer (my picks).
- **Levels:** every foe you kill drops a blue experience orb (beside the odd red health orb), worth 10 for a level 1 foe and half as much again for each level it's over that. Walk over it to pick it up and the experience bar fills. That bar runs right across the bottom of the screen in 20 sections, under the hotbar and action bar. When it's full you go up a level, with a fanfare, and the rest carries over. Level 1 takes 100 to fill, and each level after takes more (100 × level^1.4). Your level shows in a blue ring beside your health, top left, and hovering over the bar shows the numbers. Levels don't change anything else yet. Tune it in T > Levels.
- **Weapon materials:** every sword, bow, spear, axe and hammer comes in wood, bone, copper and iron. You start with wooden ones. Skeletons always leave bones where they fall (4 at a time), and wolves, frost wolves, boars and hellhounds do half the time. The second house in every town is the smithy (grey roof), with a weapon crafting table inside: use it (F / A) to make any of them: a wooden one from 10 planks, the others from 4 planks and 10 bones, copper ingots or iron ingots. A weapon's strokes and abilities are the same in every material, but its damage is multiplied by the material's factor: wood ×1, bone ×1.3, copper ×1.6, iron ×2 (tune them, and the cost, in T > Weapon materials). Each material also looks grander than the last, on the hero, the thrown spear and the icons: wood is whittled and bare; bone is bound in cord, knobbed and toothed; copper has a leather grip, a flared guard, a fuller, copper caps and recurved tips, and a red tassel on the spear; iron is longest, gold-mounted, winged and recurved, and set with a glowing gem.
- Loot on the ground glows so you can spot it: a beam of light, a pool of light and sparkles drifting up, white for crafting materials and red for health orbs (tune it in T > Loot glow). Loot that lands within a tile of a like pile joins it, so a pile has one beam however much is in it; hides lie stacked on each other.
- **F** open and close doors.
- **Camera:** hold the right mouse button and drag to turn it freely (the middle button works too). The tilt always stays at 30°. **Z** / **C** turn it a quarter at a time, and the mouse wheel zooms.
- **B** build mode: **1** floors (wood, stone), **2** walls (timber, log palisade, stone, wood fence, fieldstone wall), **3** doors (door, gate), **4** hearth. Each number opens its own row of kinds over the bar, and the numbers then pick from that row. Left click places; drag to lay a rectangle of floor, or a straight wall along whichever way you drag (as many pieces as you can pay for). A right click (without dragging) removes a piece for a full refund. Every button on the bar can be clicked too.
- **O** options (or the OPTIONS button): HDR on or off, and how bright its highlights get. Kept in this browser.
- **M** map: the mouse wheel zooms it (and the minimap) and dragging pans it. Its window has a title bar: drag that to move the window (the spot is remembered) and its X closes it, as the inventory's does. **N** on the map (or REVEAL ALL) shows the whole world, fog lifted and every name shown, without counting anything as found; N again hides it. It reopens at the zoom you left it at, centred on you. The minimap slides and turns every frame and repaints its contents a few times a second. Under the map, the NAMES buttons turn biome, water (lakes, seas, rivers), road and town names on or off one by one (kept between loads). Click a town you've seen and FAST TRAVEL TO it puts you by its well.
- Under the minimap, the name of where you are and the time of day, and under that a time-of-day strip: an old watch's sun and moon window laid out flat. The sun crosses it from sunrise (left) to sunset (right), and the moon by night, over the sky's colour now. The sunlight fades out over its last 5 seconds before sunset and the moonlight in over its first 5 (and back at dawn), and the haze's shadowed sky light with them, so the light changes over without a pop (T > Day & night: handover).
- **Region names:** every world names its land afresh. Each patch of a biome is a region (the biggest split into parts of about 15,000 tiles, crumbs folded into their neighbours), named from its biome's own words in one of three patterns: a compound and a noun (Thornwold Forest), the compound alone (the Frostspine), or an adjective and a noun (the Tangled Wilds). That gives over 1,100 names per biome, and no name repeats in a world. Walking into a region for the first time discovers it, and its name goes on the map, tinted by biome, at the point deepest inside it. When that spot would overlap a town or another name, it moves to the next deepest spot that's clear; if none is, it isn't drawn (zoomed out, smaller regions drop off). Lakes, seas, rivers and roads are named too, from their own words (Lake Silvermere, the Swiftwater, Thorngate Trail; over 1,100 names each). You can't walk into them, so coming within 4 tiles of one discovers it, and from then on its name is on the map in blue (water) or tan (roads). Map names are drawn at 16px on a canvas of their own at screen resolution, ringed with a pixel of black.
- **T** tuning panel: the tile height per level (2 by default, as tall as a wall, so a floor laid on walls would meet the ground a cliff up; 1 the old height, 0.5; above 1.25 ramps run 3 tiles long and none are a single tile, and the cliffs are cut finer with their banding kept the same size; letting go of the slider rebuilds the same world from scratch, so you lose what you've built), the camera (orthographic, or perspective with a field of view you set, and how far in and out it can zoom), the mosaic pixel size and anti-aliasing (the scene is drawn finer and averaged into each mosaic pixel, so thin lines blend instead of breaking up), the hero's light (strength by night and day, reach, colour, and how far toward the camera and how high it hangs, so the side you see stays lit), the day and night (time, clock speed, latitude, season, sun, sky and moon light, haze, exposure), sun and moon shadows (on or off, strength, reach round the view, softness, and how they weaken as the sun or moon gets low: none at 50° above the horizon, full from 60°), the health bars (how long each stays, how long the health just lost waits and how fast it shrinks, the hit flash and glow, sizes, height over the head, opacity and colours), distance haze (where it starts and thickens, how thick, and its colour by day, dusk and night), the tumble (how long it lasts, its speed going in and coming out, steering, the run speed boost after it and how long that fades over, and its cooldown), the walk (cadence, forward and backward stride, arm swing, how wide a sidestep opens, bob, lean), cloud shadows (strength, cover, softness, size, drift speed and direction), the water (clarity, body brightness, wave height and speed, river flow, sun glitter, foam), the lava (ripple height, length and speed, glow, crust, glowing cracks, breathing, drift and flow speed, pattern size, cooled crust at the banks, how many bubbles, how often they burst and how big they are, and spatter), and the black edge outline (how far back the ground behind an edge must be, and how many mosaic pixels thick).
- **G** profiler (or the GPU button), Crafting and Conquest's: the frame rate, the GPU your browser picked, the GPU time of each pass (where the browser has timestamp-query: the world, water, hero and ground haze, outline and post, shadow map, caustics, lit haze, ground haze sim, GI probes, eye adaptation, bloom) and the CPU time of each part of the frame. The Drawing line also counts the frame's draw calls and the meshes in the scene; a mesh count that climbs while you stand still means something is leaking. Under it, spike capture: turned on, it keeps any frame whose CPU time passes a limit (T > Spike capture), with every call into the renderer and the effects and each part of the game's update timed as a tree you can open down to what took the time, and by default stops the game on it until you press Resume.

### Xbox controller

Press any button on a controller and the game switches to it: the action bar, the door prompt, the help and the map show Xbox button glyphs, and a key or the mouse switches back.

- **Play:** left stick moves, right stick aims (let go and you face the way you walk), **RT** attacks (hold to keep swinging), **X** cleave, **Y** lunge (the sword's abilities), **B** dash, **A** opens and closes doors, or jumps down when you're pushing at a cliff edge you can jump from. **RB** opens the gear window, where the D-pad picks a square, **A** puts on or takes off, and **B** or **RB** closes it. Hold **LT** and the right stick turns the camera smoothly instead of aiming.
- **Build (MENU):** the right stick moves a diamond cursor around you. **RT** places (hold it and move the cursor to lay a row or an area), **LT** removes, **X** / **Y** / **A** open the floor, wall and door rows (press again to step to the next kind), **RB** hearth, **B** closes the row or leaves build mode.
- **Both:** **VIEW** map (left stick pans, **RT** / **LT** zoom, **B** closes), **L3** plot outlines, D-pad up and down zoom, left and right turn the camera to the next 45° step.

- **Sizes:** the minimap is 240px (with the place name and time of day plate under it as wide), toasts are bigger, and the gear window's squares are 96px inside, with their 3px border outside that (smaller only where the screen is too short or narrow for that), so Jordan's 48px PNG icons fill them at exactly 2x (every resource, weapon and armor piece, and the starting cloth shirt, pants and shoes; repo icons/48), never smoothed and only ever at whole multiples of their pixels (smaller screens step down a whole multiple). A dragged item is drawn at 96px too. The bar's dash, pack, build and map buttons, and the wood and stone floors, log, stucco and stone walls, wooden door, hearth, saw table, grinder, smelter, tanning rack and the armor, weapon and alchemy tables in build mode, use Jordan's painted 256px icons (icons/paint/src), shipped beside the page as lossless WebP at 64, 128 and 256 (icons/paint) and picked by srcset for the box and pixel ratio, drawn smooth; so do the starting cloth shirt, pants, boots and wooden sword (hard-alpha cutouts, filling their pack, doll and hotbar squares, and the dragged icon); the rest of the play and build bars use Jordan's 64px isometric set at 1x (repo icons/64: dash, pack, build, map, wood and stone floors, log, timber (stucco) and stone walls, hearth, saw table, grinder, smelter, tanning rack; BAR_ICON, falling back to the other icons where there's no 64). Each square is 84×100 with the button's name under the icon (ARMOR TABLE, WEAPON TABLE and FIELDSTONE shortened to fit one line). Build costs are in each button's tooltip, with what you're missing, and a piece you can't afford has its whole icon tinted red. The pack, crafting list and touch dash button keep the 48px icons. Empty gear squares show Jordan's 48px slot markers with the slot's name under the square, and the windows use his palette (panel #282430, edges #4e425f, squares #211e26, labels #a69ab3) so the icons' black outlines read against them, with its text scaled to match. The crafting window is half as big again. Icons are drawn at twice the resolution of their pixel maps: doubled with Scale2x (rounding off diagonal steps), lit along their top edges, shaded along their bottom ones and outlined a pixel wide (iconArt).

## The world

- **Terrain:** eight height levels over open water. Each level is one tile high by default (half a tile if you set it in the tuning panel). The lowlands are broad, gentle country. Most of the steep ground is gathered into a few mountain ranges. Terraces are at least three tiles deep (no ground stands more than a level over anything within two tiles of it), so steep ground comes out as a few broad terraces, not a staircase, and the side of every terrace is a slope that reaches half a tile into the lower ground, so you can't walk up it. Cliff faces are rough rock rather than smooth ramps: they bulge into buttresses, are cut by gullies and stepped into ledges, and are banded with strata. The lip wanders and crumbles and the foot spreads out into scree, while the ground on either side keeps its own colour right up to the rock. Only a few rocks of the local kind (mossy stone, sandstone ledges, snow-capped boulders) lie on the faces and at their feet. Banks under water stay smooth. The meadows roll gently and climb mostly by ramps, while the snowy heights are steeper, with sheer terraces and few ramps. Ramps, two tiles long wherever there's room, join the terraces. They are always at least two tiles wide, and always an even number of tiles wide. After the ramps are carved, a flood fill from the first town adds more ramps until every stretch of land you could walk to is reachable.
- **Lakes, rivers and canyons:** about forty lakes fill hollows (ground that lies low for its neighbourhood), each to just under the lowest bank round it, with a sandy shelf round the edge that shelves away into hollows and deeps of varying depth. Two dozen rivers rise at springs on the tops of the heights and wind downhill into a lake (or, if none is in reach, the sea). Their courses come from a search out from the lakes and the sea that only ever climbs, so a river never runs uphill. It is cheapest along the winding lines of a warped noise, so rivers meander, and dear along the brink of a drop, so they go over a cliff head on, as a fall, instead of running along its top. Rivers that meet join. A river lies level across its width and widens as it goes; where it drops a level its water falls in white rapids. Every water surface stays at or under the ground round it, checked again once the land is finished, so water never stands over its banks. Seven deep canyons wind through the high ground, most of them across the way between two towns. Nothing can cross water or a canyon except over a bridge.
- **Beaches:** wherever land meets a lake or the sea at the water's own level (along a side or round a corner; not rivers, swamp pools or lava), that land tile is a beach: a shallow slope a tile wide running down to the water's edge, sand by the sea and brown dirt (blending into the ground beside it) by a lake. A straight shore is one plain slope; a point of land comes down in hips and a bay's inside corner in a valley, the way a roof's corners do, and neighbouring beaches meet point for point. Off every beach two rows of shoal (dirt in a lake) carry it on down under the water at the same slope, walkable right out to the drop-off where the bank falls away as it always did (none within 10 tiles of a dock, where the boats moor). The swells over them (and by a lake's beach) are lowered by their full height so a crest only reaches the still water's line. The light is smoothed over beaches and shoals where they meet each other and the land (T > Ground patches: Beaches smooth and span). Nothing grows or is built on a beach, and it isn't part of any plot. Towns, roads, ramps, bridges, floors, farms, grottos and anything placed on a tile keep the old bank. On the map beaches are sand or brown, shoals cyan. `GAME.beaches()` counts them.
- **Biomes:** Bone Road's four (the Whispering Wood, Mirefen Bog, the Hollow Graves, and towns like Emberbrook), plus Goldenreach Meadows, the Sunscorch Waste desert, and the Frostspine snow on the high ground.
- **Waterfall spray:** wherever running water steps down a level, spray and mist rise off the foot of the fall: soft additive puffs lit by the sky, the sun (through its shadow) and the torch, worked out entirely in the vertex shader from each puff's seed and the time, and soft where they meet the water, ground or cliffs (they fade out against the world's depth instead of cutting off). T > Waterfall spray sets amount, puff size, spawn rate, lifetime and colour.
- **Smoke:** the wastes' vents and the hearths' chimneys smoke the same way: soft round puffs worked out in the vertex shader, lit and fogged like the spray, rising, leaning off with the wind and thinning away, blended over the world (so they can be darker than it). The vents' smoke is sulphur yellow and orange with a little glow of its own, the hearths' grey. T > Smoke sets amount, puff size, spawn rate, lifetime, rise, wind, the vents' glow and the colours.
- **Ore rocks:** copper and iron rocks stand among the rock pillars wherever those grow, about one copper rock to every three pillars and one iron rock to every five. Each is a pillar of the local stone with tapered, four-sided chunks of the metal leaning out of it all round (after Crafting and Conquest's ore), and a couple more broken off at its foot. Hitting one gives copper ore or iron ore, and no stone. T > Resources scales everything you gather or that drops (wood, stone, ore and hides) by 0.5×, 1×, 2× or 4×. T > Ore rocks sets the metals' colours, how many chunks each rock has, and their size. Ore is gated by weapon material: copper rocks need a bone weapon or better and iron rocks a copper one or better; with anything less (fists or wood for copper) they only shake and ring, and a toast says what's needed.
- **Danger bands:** once the world is made, every tile's straight-line distance to the nearest town is cut into 10 bands, each holding a tenth of the land (band 1 nearest the towns, 10 farthest; on seed 12345 the edges fall at 63, 91, 113, 133, 151, 171, 196, 227 and 264 tiles). Stone grows everywhere, but each metal's rocks only from its band out: copper 2, iron 4, Verdane 6, Caerite 7, Orivane 8, Auralith 9 (ORE_BAND). A monster's level is the band it spawns in, shown as a number in a ring left of its health bar (30px over its head, 40px by the big bar at the top while you point at it); levels don't change its strength yet. The map's BANDS button is a debug view that colours the bands green (1) through violet (10), with the fog off.
- **Fantasy metals:** past iron come Verdane (green), Caerite (blue), Orivane (purple) and Auralith (prismatic, like abalone shell: its ore chunks, armor, weapons and dropped ore and ingots shimmer round the rainbow as you see them from different angles, swirled across the surface and drifting slowly, and its rock's light cycles through the same colours; T > Auralith shimmer), each rarer than the last (per rock pillar in the bands where each grows: copper 1/3, iron 1/5, Verdane 1/6, Caerite 1/7, Orivane 1/8, Auralith 1/9; v310 raised the top four from 1/10, 1/18, 1/32, 1/56, which left only about 45 Auralith rocks in the world; seed 12345 now has about 1000 Verdane, 710 Caerite, 470 Orivane and 270 Auralith). Every ore rock shares the copper/iron chunk shape and differs only in colour, so none can be mistaken for a plant. The metal chunks are polished (a tight shine that catches the light at narrow angles), and Verdane and up each cast a short point light of their colour on the ground around them (range 1.6), stronger at night (T > Ore rocks: glow per metal, range, metal shine). Each needs a weapon of the metal before it (Verdane an iron one), and the smelter makes its ore into ingots. Each metal's ingots make a sword, bow and spear at a weapon crafting table (4 planks and 10 ingots; damage Verdane ×2.4, Caerite ×2.9, Orivane ×3.5, Auralith ×4.2) and a set of armor at an armor crafting table (the same leather and ingot costs as the other sets; armor per chest, legs, boots, head: Verdane 6/5/3/3, Caerite 7/5/3/4, Orivane 8/6/4/4, Auralith 9/7/4/5; my picks). On the hero the weapons are the iron ones made in the metal, its fittings in a paler shade and its gem glowing in the ore's colour, each tier grander: Caerite adds outer guard wings, limb plates and spear collars, Orivane blade, bow-tip and spearhead flanges, Auralith a crowned pommel, fanned grip plates and a crescent spearhead. The armor sets wear the iron plate pieces in the metal's colours, with shoulder plates, doubled from Orivane up. All their icons are Jordan's 48px set.
- **Rounded ground patches:** the ground's quarter-tile squares of colour are drawn with wavy, rounded edges. The ground's shader takes each point's colour from a quarter a little way off, pushed about by smooth noise, when that quarter is joined to it (on the same level, or up or down a ramp; from a quarter-tile colour texture filled as chunks are built), so paths, streets and ramps melt into the ground round them as biomes do, and nothing bleeds over a cliff. T > Ground patches turns it off, sets how far the edges wander and how tight the wiggles are, and blends the colours into each other (at 1, one continuous noisy gradient with no edges).
- **Deep snow and sand:** snowfields and deserts lie under a layer of snow or sand, knee to waist deep, rising and falling in drifts and dunes. Whatever walks through it ploughs a trench, heaping what it pushes aside into a rim along either side, and the trench slowly fills back in (a compute map round you, `src/gfx/drift.js`, like the trample map). You and the foes wade in it, slowed the deeper it is. The layer is one grid mesh round the view, lifted in its vertex shader by the depth, the trench and the rim; it thins out at paths, cliffs, water, buildings and biome edges, and leaves a hollow round trees and rocks. T > Deep snow and sand sets the depths, the size of the drifts, the trench's depth, rim and width, how long it takes to fill in, whether foes plough it, and how much snow and sand each slow you. It also has smooth normals (blending the faceted look into smooth shading) and a switch that takes the layer right up to cliff edges, at full depth to the lip and the foot, instead of thinning out before them, and one that lays it over ramps too, following their slope down from level to level.
- **Grass:** meadows, forest floors, town yards, swamps, the deep forest and graveyards are covered in tufts of grass, with flowers among them and reeds in the swamps (in the pools too). None of it is a mesh: one instanced draw makes every tuft up in its vertex shader from a hash of its tile, as Crafting and Conquest's grass.js does, within a window round the middle of the view. A texture a texel a tile over the world (filled as chunks are built or changed) says which quarters of each tile are flat open ground and what ground it is; paths, streets, floors, bridges, slopes, cliff edges and the feet of trees and rocks grow none. They bend through the same trample map and wind as the plants below. They get no depth outline (each tuft marks its pixels with alpha 0 for the outline pass), so taller grass doesn't turn into strokes. T > Grass has the density, the share of grass, flowers and reeds, how far out they're drawn, their sizes, lean, shading and colours, and a switch to give them the outline back. Ramps grow grass too, the tufts standing upright and each lifted to the slope under it, and the ground's light is smoothed over a ramp's crest and foot (T > Ground patches: rampSmooth, rampSpan) so they round off instead of creasing. Each flower's head is a little double pyramid; T > Grass sets how many sides it has round (4 to 12, rounder as they go up) and how tall its peak stands (below 0 the middle sinks and the head is a cup).
- **Cutting grass:** the sword cuts grass as it goes: a swing cuts an arc in front, cleave (and the buzzsaw) a ring and the lunge a lane. The cuts go into a cut map (a second copy of the snow trench compute pass) that the grass reads at each tuft's foot, so cut grass drops to stubs and flowers and reeds lose their heads; it grows back over time. Fresh cuts throw up bits of green blade that flutter down. T > Grass has cut (on/off), stub (the height left), regrow (seconds) and bits (how many).
- **Plants that bend:** ferns and bushes lean away from whatever walks through them (you, and the foes near you, but not ghosts) and stand back up behind it over a second or two; cleave and the buzzsaw sweep them round you, and they all sway in gusts of wind that run across the ground. What's walking presses into a trample map, a compute pass over a grid round you (`src/gfx/trample.js`, 4 cells a tile, 40 tiles across) that holds which way the plants there lean and how far, easing back upright each frame; each piece of a plant is drawn turned about the foot of its own stem by that lean, so a blade's tip swings over and down while its foot stays put. T > Plants has the lean, how long they take to recover, how wide a berth you push, whether foes push them, and the wind.
- **Water apart:** swamp pools and lava never touch water. Each connected body of water and lava runs all one or the other, whichever most of it already is, and swamp pools stay at least a tile away from both.
- **Swamp pools:** the bog's pools are sunk knee deep, with muddy beds and smooth banks sloping down into them. You (and anything else) can wade through them, slowly, and the murky water swallows your legs. Wading makes real waves: a small sheet of water is simulated round you (src/gfx/ripples.js), which your body pushes aside and your legs stir (and so do foes wading near you, except ghosts), and its waves run out, bounce off the banks and die away, their crests drawn like the bank ripples. Their water is still and murky, with its own simpler shader (no waves, glitter or caustics) and its own section in the tuning panel, Swamp water: colour, clarity, body brightness, and the ripples running off the banks (how strong, how fast, how far out), and your wake (how strong, how fast its waves run, how fast they die away, how wide its crest lines are), each with its own colour. Legs stir the water only while walking, so it settles when you stand still, and any swell left standing in a pool sinks away.
- **Steady glows:** the eye's exposure is taken back off the loot glows, the hero's light and the blue see-through silhouette, so they look as bright in a dark wood at night as in the open (each has a Steady slider in T, 1 all the way, 0 brightening in the dark like everything else).
- **Swamp trees:** the bare trees in the swamps are gnarled, mossy live oaks: a flared foot with moss on it, a kinked trunk, and three to five wide limbs that reach out, rise and then sag, ending in bare twigs, with clumps of moss along them and grey-green Spanish moss hanging off in strands that sway in the wind. T > Trees > Swamp tree size scales them.
- **The Elderwood (deep forest):** the heart of any wood big enough, about ten tiles in from its edge, so it's always ringed by ordinary forest. Its floor is brown-green with fallen needles. Redwoods stand on a loose grid, one to a 4 × 4 block at most. Each trunk is one uneven surface: a foot that splays into a few lopsided buttress roots, which climb the trunk as fading flutes, with streaky bark and a slight wander and twist. They can't be felled, and nobody can claim plots there. Their trunks run off the top of the view. Like walls when you're indoors, every trunk nearer the camera than you snaps down to a stump with a ragged, broken top and dark heartwood inside, so you can still tell it's there, and its shadow still falls whole. The crowns are up out of sight, just above the top of the view: they draw nothing but cast the sun's and moon's shadows. Under them grow ferns, toadstools, logs and the odd young pine. Giant spiders run in twos and threes. Mantraps are rooted: they turn to face you, and once you come within a few tiles they rear back and lunge their heads out to bite.
- **The Blasted Wastes:** burned country like the graves but far more broken, with steeper rise and fall. Its ash-grey ground is strewn with charred pumice and black, burned trees with embers still glowing in their cracks. Smoking vents, holes two tiles across with a lip of ash and yellow sulphur, breathe smoke near you; nothing walks into them and they can't be mined. Craters are punched into it, and it has more bottomless canyons than anywhere else. Lava lies in pools in the crater floors and the hollows, sunk into their banks like small lakes, and lakes and rivers there run with lava too, so none of it can be walked into (a lava river can be bridged). The lava glows by night: a dark crust drifts over the melt, cracked with bright seams, cooling to crust at the banks and carried along a lava river's flow. Slow ripples lift its surface, and bubbles swell up and burst in a flash and a ring of spatter. Monsters keep out of it and roads go round it. Hellhounds (charcoal wolves, horned, with burning eyes and a mane, tail tip and paws of flame) and floating fire spirits haunt it. No towns stand there.
- **Roads:** roads join the towns and three waystones in every biome. They wind round rougher ground instead of running dead straight, and run diagonally as well as straight (a diagonal step is two tiles round a corner, so the road is always joined side to side). They only change level by ramps, cross rivers and canyons on plank bridges (piled into the river bed, or hung from rope rails over a canyon; where two bridges run side by side they make one wide bridge, with no rails between them and the boards meeting), a street only runs out to the edge of town where it carries on as a road (one that can't get out, past cliffs or water, stops at the last door on it), and every road ends at a town or a waystone.
- **Health bars:** point at a monster (the mouse over it, or on a controller the one you aim at), or hurt it by any means, and a health bar shows over its head, staying 3 seconds after. Its name and a large health bar show at the top centre of the screen too, as in V Rising, but only while you point at it. A hit flashes both bars, and the health just lost stays as a pale part that flares white and shrinks away.
- **Towns:** about eleven per world (at least 120 tiles apart), each with a plaza, a well, streets and four houses built from the same walls, doors, floors and roofs you build with. Each town is fenced round wherever its edge can be walked across, in wood or dry stone, with a gate standing open where each street runs out into a road. A town no road reaches still gets one gate at the end of a street. Monsters keep out of town, and you wake by the well in Emberbrook if you fall.
- **Townsfolk:** every town has its own people, the same ones each visit: about one to a house (half of one or two in a hamlet, two in a medium town and two or three in a large one), each a seeded townsperson with a job that fits the town (the tannery's tanner, the smithy's smith, the cathedral's clergy). Come within 40 tiles of a town and its people are there, strolling the streets between house fronts, the fountain, lamps, flower boxes and street corners, standing about a while, talking when two stand together, sweeping if it's their job. They step out of your way (you never bump into them), and some wave as you pass. Point at one to see their name and job at the top of the screen. T > Townsfolk tunes them. They keep a day by the clock: up between about 5:30 and 7, at work 8 to 5 with an hour off at noon (each workshop's keeper at their bench, the clergy in the cathedral, the sweeper, crier and busker out on the streets), on errands the rest of the day, home a little before bed and asleep in their own bed at night (upstairs in a two-story house: they walk the stairs), lying down under the blanket and getting up again in the morning. At night the streets are empty but for the watch and the lamplighter. They open doors to go through and shut them behind them, and a town you arrive at has everyone where the hour says. Press F beside one to hear what they have to say.
- **Farms:** the meadows' points of interest, one for every so much meadow (none in a small one, up to three in a big one). Each has a farmhouse of timber walls on a plank floor, about 4 × 5 tiles with one corner of its back left out, under its own roof. Its door faces the gate of the wooden fence round its yard. Beside the yard lie two wheat fields of 4 to 6 tiles a side on tilled earth: dark soil ridged in four rows a tile, with the wheat standing on the ridges. Nobody can claim a farm as a plot or take its walls down. The wheat is drawn with the grass, each clump a stalk with an ear at the top and leaves lower down. Whatever cuts grass reaps it a tile at a time. Each ripe tile you reach gives 1 wheat and drops to stubble, which grows back over 90 seconds, green turning gold, before it can be reaped again. T > Wheat sets its height, density, stubble, regrowth time, yield and colours.
- **Fairy grottos:** the deep forest's points of interest, one for every so much of it (up to three). Each is a knee-deep pool, about 6 × 4, at the foot of a cliff and well away from any lake or river. A waterfall spills over a mossy slab between boulders on the cliff's top, arcs down into the pool and throws up spray, and a trickle or two runs down the face beside it. Moss covers the boulders and drapes over the lip, with russet strands trailing down the rock, ferns grow thick round the bank and up on the rock, and toadstools glow round the edge. The water is clear teal and glows from within, swelling softly, with motes of light drifting under its surface, and it lights the place round it. You wade through it as through a swamp pool, the wake rippling out. Lily pads, some with flowers, float on it and drift aside as you wade past, then settle back. Fairies, little lights in pink, blue, lilac and gold with gauzy fluttering wings, loop and hover over it. T > Fairy grotto sets the water, its glow and light, and the waterfall; the fairies are under T > Ambient life.

Each is a tall, tapering obelisk of weathered sandstone with a pyramid cap, on a 3 × 2 patch, sunk a good way into the sand and leaning a little, turned a little off square. Every face is carved, through a normal map rather than raised pieces, with a sunken frame and two columns of glyphs: bars, eyes, suns, birds, zigzags, ankh-like crosses and waves, a few worn away, with the grooves a shade darker. ## Building

The wilds outside town are marked out into plots, as in V Rising: about 450 a world, each some 35 to 50 tiles across. Each one lies on a single terrace and stops at cliffs, water, roads and towns, so nearly every plot is flat ground to build on. A plot may also take in a knoll or dip beside it that is too small to be a plot of its own. Steep or broken ground that no plot fits stays unclaimable. Press **P** (or the map's PLOTS button) to show their outlines on the map and minimap, white until claimed; hovering over one there, or on the ground in build mode, washes the whole plot white. Nothing can be built on a plot until you claim it with a hearth (4 stone, 2 wood): a round stone firepit under a hood, with a chimney that rises clear of any roof built over it. You can lay a floor under it. Build a wall (not a door) on one side of it and it becomes a fireplace built into that wall, with jambs, a mantel and a chimney breast. It stays on that wall while the wall stands. If that wall comes down, it moves to another wall only when just one is left round it, and it turns back into the round pit once there are no walls round it. A claimed plot turns blue. You can have one hearth. **Before you own a plot (v318)** the build bar shows only the hearth (and exit); the rest of it appears once you've claimed one. Meanwhile, in build mode, every unclaimed plot near you is outlined on the ground by a low white wall of light, bright at its foot and fading out as it rises, with faint streaks drifting up it (T > Plot walls sets its height, brightness, fade, streaks and how far out it's drawn). **v319:** in that same state the map and minimap show the plot outlines (as P does), and the ground round you (45 tiles) is tinted green on plot tiles and red off them, not over water, fading out toward the edge (T > Plot walls: tint strength and reach). Once you have a plot, build mode outlines only yours, with the same wall in blue (v320). Standing near it heals you, you wake beside it when you fall, and taking it down gives the plot up.

Floors need flat ground that no slope reaches into. Walls (not doors) can also run along the top of a one-level cliff: only the ground on top has to be clear and flat, the rocks along that edge make way, and the wall stands on the high side, overhanging the slope a little. Wood floors cost 2 wood, stone floors 2 stone, timber walls 2 wood and 2 stone, log palisade walls 4 wood, stone walls 4 stone, doors 5 wood, wood fences 1 wood, fieldstone walls 2 stone, gates 3 wood, iron fences 2 iron ore, iron gates 3 iron ore, iron doors 6 iron ore. Iron fences are wrought-iron railings with spear-topped bars, iron gates swing the same bars between capped iron posts, and iron doors are banded, studded iron plates in a door frame. Fences, fieldstone walls and gates (wood or iron) stand half height. They block the way like walls and doors, but they never close in a room: no roof goes up, and inside a fenced yard you're still outdoors. A gate opens and shuts like a door (**F**), and neither gates nor fences turn a hearth into a fireplace. Close in a room with walls and doors (any shape, up to about 500 tiles) and a hipped roof goes up over it. Rooms that meet across a wall or door, like a room walled off inside a bigger one, make one building under one roof. The roof hides while you stand anywhere in the building, and every roof hides in build mode. Inside, every wall of the building you're in (your room and any rooms joined to it) that is both lower on the screen than you and between you and the camera, and every outer wall whose inside faces away from the camera, drops to waist height so you can see in. Doors always stand full height. You heal faster under a roof, and monsters can't get through walls or closed doors. They can't strike you through one either (or across a cliff). A monster that has noticed you finds its way round walls, water and cliffs to reach you. If there's no way to you, say you've shut yourself indoors, it loses interest, heads back to where it came from, and pays you no mind for a few seconds unless you hit it. Stone walls are mortared solid, so nothing shows through the joints.

## How it renders

The Dungeon Golf pipeline: a low-resolution buffer scaled up by a whole number, an orthographic camera at a fixed 30° tilt (starting at a 45° turn, where tile edges are a clean 2:1 pixel staircase), and a camera snapped to whole big pixels with the leftover slid in CSS. With the outline on, the frame renders to a target with a depth texture, and a pass on the low-res buffer strokes any pixel whose neighbours within the outline's thickness lie more than the outline distance further back, as Crafting and Conquest's toon strokes do, so strokes are whole mosaic pixels. Scenery is merged into vertex-coloured, flat-shaded meshes, and the hero draws in a second pass so a silhouette shows through anything in front.

The 1216 × 1216 map is split into 32 × 32-tile chunks. Only chunks near the camera are built, nearest first and a few milliseconds' worth per frame, and far ones are let go. A chunk is built in small steps so no one frame pays for much of it: its ground four rows of tiles at a time (then its bog, water and lava), then what stands on it eight rows at a time, then its meshes one at a time, then its building pieces and grass. Felling a tree or building a wall rebuilds only its chunk.

## Global illumination

Crafting and Conquest's probe lighting, made for a height map instead of voxels (`src/gfx/probes.js`). Every chunk works out a block of a height field, a texel every half tile: the ground with whatever stands on it (rocks, trunks, walls), plus one floating layer above it for tree crowns and roofs, which leaves let some light through and roofs don't. The blocks of the chunks round the camera sit in one texture. Probes stand in columns, one a tile, at four heights over the ground (0.4, 1.4, 3.2 and 7 tiles). The grid follows the camera and is sized to reach as far as the widest zoom shows. Each frame a compute pass traces only the probes inside the current view, as the camera is turned and zoomed, and only those that need it: new to the grid, near ground that changed, or (with bounce light) due a refresh. A chunk's block is worked out over frames, a couple of milliseconds a frame by the clock, while the old one stands. Rays march through the height field. Lit surfaces blend the probes of the four columns round them, two heights each, and fade out probes that have a cliff or a wall between them and the surface, gradually, so no hard line shows on the ground.

The tuning panel's Global illumination section has the switch, and `L` cycles it:

- **0, off:** the plain sky light, as before.
- **1, sky visibility (the default):** the sky's light, less wherever ground, walls, roofs or leaves hide the sky (under trees, in canyons, indoors).
- **2, bounce light:** all the light arriving, the sky's plus what the ground and things round about give back of the sun or moon (with a shadow ray), the lamps and the torch, and lava's glow. It bounces on through the probes' own light.

There are also sliders for strength, a minimum of the plain light to keep, rays and their reach, light through leaves, bounce strength and rays, and how many probes are relit each frame.

## Lit haze

Lit haze marched per pixel (`src/gfx/haze.js`). The game draws at a low mosaic resolution, so each frame, after the scene is drawn, a compute pass marches a ray through the air for every mosaic pixel. The ray runs from about 14 tiles above you down to just below the ground in view, and stops at what that pixel sees. It takes even steps with no random jitter, so the haze has no grid to show and no dithering, and it never smears across an edge. The haze is thickest on the bare ground (trunks, walls and rocks don't count, so no column of haze stands on a tall trunk) and thins with height, broken into slow wisps that drift with the clouds. It's lit by:

- the sun or moon through its shadow map, so tall trees and buildings throw shafts through it, scattered more forward than back;
- the sky, by how much of it the GI probes see from there (with bounce light, all the light arriving there);
- the lamps and your torch;
- lava's glow: a second compute pass blurs what the ground gives off into a glow map each frame, smoothly within a tile as well as across them, and the glow fades with height.

The outline pass lays the result over the picture. The tuning panel's Lit haze section has the switch and the sliders, including the number of steps. By default the march runs once for each 2×2 block of mosaic pixels, a quarter of the rays. It goes out to the block's farthest depth and keeps what it had at the nearest too. A second pass then gives each pixel a blend of the four blocks round it, by nearness and by how well their depths match its own. So flat ground blends smoothly and an edge between near and far keeps its own haze on each side. The slider "One ray per this many pixels" set to 1 marches every pixel as before. One of them, "Light it adds over things" (lift), scales only the light the haze adds, not how much it hides. Turned down, it keeps shadows and near things dark while far things still fade. It sits on top of the distance haze, which still fades the far ground to the sky's colour.

## Ground haze

Neon Haze's liquid ground haze (`src/gfx/groundhaze.js`): a thin haze lying on the ground in swamps (green), graveyards (white) and the Blasted Wastes (orange). It's a 2D fluid sim in compute over a 48-tile grid that follows the camera, 4 cells a tile. Each frame it advects the air and the haze, eases the air back to a gentle wind (the clouds' way) and the haze back to a patchy resting field that drifts with it, and adds your wake. It also puts back the swirls the grid smears away and solves for pressure so the flow stays incompressible. Trees, rocks and other solid things are walls it flows round. The game fills a site map round the camera every few tiles, a couple of milliseconds a frame: the ground's height (the top of water and lava, and draped over cliffs as steep ramps, so the haze covers the rock faces), what's solid, and how much haze rests there and in what colour, by biome (in swamps thickest over and along the pools and in the Wastes over the lava, thinning away from them; none lies over lakes and rivers, or inside a closed-in building), blurred so it fades in at the edges. The haze is drawn after you and the swamp water, so it lies over the pools, as stacked slices that follow the ground, lit like everything else (sky and GI probes, the sun or moon through its shadow, the lamps and your torch) and fogged. The haze is read from the sim through a cubic B-spline rather than plain bilinear filtering, and a smoothing step mixes a little of each cell's neighbours into the air's motion each frame (T > Ground haze > Smoothing; the haze itself isn't smoothed, so your wake stays clear), so ripples a cell or two across never build up and the sim's grid doesn't show. The tuning panel's Ground haze section has each biome's amount and colour, and the sim's and the look's sliders.

## Eye adaptation and HDR

The scene is drawn into float targets, so light brighter than white survives until the picture is shown. Each frame a compute pass (`src/gfx/exposure.js`) measures how bright the picture is, haze included: the geometric mean of its mosaic pixels, weighted toward the middle of the screen. The exposure then eases toward what brings that to a target, the way your eyes adjust. It darkens quickly when you step out of the deep forest onto a sunlit plain and opens up slowly again, and by default it never goes brighter than the look as tuned. The exposure stays on the GPU, where the outline pass applies it. The tuning panel's Eye adaptation section shows the brightness it measures and the exposure it's using, and has the targets (one by day and a lower one by night, eased between as the sun sets and rises, so night isn't brought up to day), the limits, the speeds and the middle weighting. It sits on top of the day and night auto exposure.

HDR (the options menu, **O**) switches the canvas to float with extended tone mapping. On an HDR display, highlights (the sun on things, lava, lit haze) then ease off above white toward a peak you set, rather than clipping. Off, or on a display without HDR, everything over white clips as before.

The options menu also has **Show frame rate**: frames a second and the frame's time in milliseconds, under the GPU button, with none of the profiler's measuring behind it.

## Bloom and the grade

Bloom (`src/gfx/bloom.js`): each frame, after the haze and the eye adaptation, a compute pass keeps whatever is brighter than a threshold at half the mosaic's size, eased in so nothing pops. Two more passes blur it with a Gaussian, and the outline pass adds it back as a soft glow round the sun's ring, lava, lamps and bright shafts. Last of all comes the grade:

- a black point, taken out of every colour (white stays white), so whatever the haze lifted goes dark again;
- contrast round a pivot brightness;
- saturation.

The tuning panel's Picture section has all of it.

## Crowds

Foes are drawn in crowds, so a horde costs about what one foe does. Each kind's build is made once and cut into pieces: the parts that move together under one joint (the body, or something the pose moves on its own, such as a jaw, a flame, a leg or the mantrap's stalk), merged by material. Each piece is one instanced draw, with a copy for each foe of that kind, so the draw calls stay the same however many foes there are (about 100 pieces across all 14 kinds, plus one draw for every blob shadow). A foe keeps only its joints, which the pose code moves as before, and their places are copied into the pieces before each frame is drawn, farthest first so see-through foes blend in order. When a foe dies, each part of its build flies off on its own as before. Foes push apart only from neighbours on nearby tiles, rather than every foe checking every other.

T > Crowd test keeps extra foes round you (up to 1,000 over the usual 10 by day and 18 by night) and shows how many there are, the draw calls a frame and the frame rate.

## The drawing library (src/gfx)

Emberwild draws with raw WebGPU through a small library of its own, shaped like the parts of three.js the game was first written on so the game code reads the same:

- `math.js`: vectors, colours, quaternions, Euler angles, matrices, planes, rays and view frustums.
- `scene.js`: the scene graph (objects nested in groups, each placed, turned and sized), meshes and instanced meshes (one mesh drawn many times in one call, each copy placed by its own matrix), haze, orthographic and perspective cameras, and the sky, sun and point lights.
- `geometry.js`: vertex buffers and the primitive shapes (boxes, cylinders, cones, the round solids, discs, rings, planes, lathes).
- `materials.js`: lit (standard), unlit (basic) and normal materials, materials with their own WGSL (`ShaderMaterial`: uniforms, textures and extra vertex attributes), textures and render targets.
- `shaders.js`: the WGSL every pipeline shares: the frame and object uniform blocks, haze, flat face normals, cloud shadow, the sun's 17-tap PCF shadow and the lighting (sky light, sun, point lights, Lambert plus a GGX specular lobe), and the built-in materials.
- `probes.js`: probe lighting over a height field (see Global illumination).
- `haze.js`: lit haze, marched per pixel (see Lit haze).
- `exposure.js`: eye adaptation (see Eye adaptation and HDR).
- `bloom.js`: bloom (see Bloom and the grade).
- `groundhaze.js`: the liquid ground haze (see Ground haze).
- `particles.js`: spell and world effects (see Spell effects): GPU-stepped particles in a ring of slots, drawn see-through and solid, and FxRunner, which plays effects written as phases.
- `renderer.js`: draws a scene into the canvas or a render target: the sun's shadow map first when it's due, then everything the camera's layers see, solid things nearest first and see-through things farthest first, culled to the view. All of a frame's passes go in one command buffer.

The water, lava, trunk cutaway and outline shaders are WGSL, inline in `index.html`.

## Water

Crafting and Conquest's water surface. Fresnel blends the sky's reflection with the water body (light scattered back up out of it), sun glitter comes off the wave slopes, and whatever lies under the surface shows through the water above it, each colour absorbed at its own rate (red first). A lace of foam runs along the shore, and the water washes it in and out. The waves are scrolling slope noise instead of an FFT ocean. Rivers carry the same water along their flow, using a flow map with two phases half a cycle apart so the stretch never builds up, streaked with foam. The surface is drawn in two passes over what's below: one multiplies it by what the water lets through, then the next adds the water's own light. The sea uses it too, and shelves off from the coast. Because the camera looks down from high above, where Fresnel shows little sky and the sun's glitter is mostly off screen, the water also has touches that read from up there. Slow swells lift the surface itself and lap up and down the banks, sparkles twinkle on them, and caustics play over the bed in the shallows. The caustics are real ones (`src/gfx/caustics.js`, Evan Wallace's method, as in Ultra-Water): each frame a fine grid over one tile of the waves has the sun refracted through the wave slopes onto a bed under it, and each landed triangle adds how much it shrank, so the light the waves gather into bright lines and the dark between them comes from the waves themselves. They are laid on the bed where your line of sight through the water meets it (found from the depth of the world under the water), traced back up toward the sun to where that light came in, so they lie along the slope of the bank and shift with its depth, and they light the bed rather than glow on the surface. The bed under the water is also bent by the waves: the world drawn so far is copied before the water (`renderer.snapshot`), and the water shows the copy a little off where the waves tilt, checking the copy's depth so nothing standing above the water is pulled into it. The swells are smooth shaded. The hero's torch lights the water but doesn't glint on it. Each of these has a slider in the tuning panel's Water section, along with ripple size, how sharply the caustics focus, how deep under the waves they land, and how much the waves bend the bed. There are also sliders for light and shade on the swells, a sheen on their crests, turquoise shallows, and a crisp foam line at the shore with ripples running out from it. Those four are off by default.

## Day, night and clouds

The day and night follow Crafting and Conquest's sky without drawing it: the sun is placed by the hour, latitude, season and north; its light reddens and dims through the air at a low sun (Rayleigh and haze extinction over the air mass); the sky's light shades from night through dusk to day; the moon, running the sun's own arc twelve hours behind (so it climbs as high by night as the sun by day), lights the night; and the exposure opens up as the light fails. Cloud shadows are a tiling noise laid over the world and slowly scrolled, dimming only the sun's or moon's light in every lit material's shader, so lamps and the torch are untouched.

## Build queue

Floors, walls, fences, doors and gates you can't pay for when you lay them (in reach, on your claimed plot) are queued instead of refused: the preview shows white, and a queued piece stays as a see-through white preview. Queued pieces go up one at a time, every 0.35 s, in the order they were laid for each material: a piece waits until the crates, chests and stations on its plot (outputs, then inputs) hold what it costs, and every piece after it that needs any of the same materials waits too. A piece needing only other materials goes ahead (a stone wall queued after a wood floor goes up as soon as there's stone, even with no planks). A new piece you lay that needs something the queue is waiting on joins the queue behind it. Its cost is taken from there, never from your pack.

**Plot stores.** Standing on your claimed plot, everything in its crates, chests and stations counts as yours for building and crafting (build costs, tooltips, red tints and the crafting lists all count it). A cost is taken from the pack first, then crates and chests, then station outputs, then station inputs. A piece only queues when the pack and the plot together can't pay for it. REMOVE on a queued piece takes it off the queue; building the piece there yourself also clears it.

## Spell effects (v565)

Particles stepped on the GPU (`src/gfx/particles.js`), for spells and for the world's own effects. The CPU writes a particle into a ring of slots once, when it's spawned (where, how fast, how long it lives, how it moves and looks); a compute pass steps every live one each frame (gravity or lift, drag, a pull toward its anchor, a swirl round it, turbulence, the wind, a bounce off its own floor), and two draws read the same buffer: everything soft or glowing in one premultiplied pass (a particle can add light, cover what's behind it, or both), and solid bits in another (lit, outlined, dithering away at the end of their life).

Particle styles: glow (embers, motes, fireflies), spark (stretched along its speed, a hot head and cooling tail), glint (a twinkle), puff (smoke, dust, vapour, lit as a ball in bands), flame (self-lit, cooling down its ramp, turning into smoke part way), ring and disc (lying on the ground: shock fronts, scorches, pools of light), beam (a bolt segment), and solid shards, chunks, drops and crystals that shoot up out of the ground and sink back.

Colours come from ramps (FX_RAMPS: fire, frost, arcane, holy, heal, poison, storm, blood, water, shadow, earth, smoke, dust), dark to bright, read in hard steps; each particle walks along its ramp over its life.

Effects (FX_LIB in index.html) are written as phases, each with emitters (a burst as it begins or a rate through it, from a point, sphere, ring, disc, box or line, a spreading front with `grow`), a light that swells or a flash that dies away, and moments for the game (a hit event, screen shake, a short hit-stop). A phase can loop until the effect is stopped, and can move a 'head' point across it (a thrown fireball). The first ones: frost nova, fireball, lightning, heal, poison cloud, campfire, splash and bleed.

The dust puffs and chips that hits, falls and building throw up, lava spitting and bleeding wounds use it too. T > Spell effects tunes it (density, glow, size, hard steps, smoke banding, effect lights, shake, hit-stop) and casts each effect in front of you. The ideas were learnt from pixelfx and Sprite Fusion's pixel effects (choreography in phases, light over flat fills, ramps that cool); the code is our own.

## Life in the air

Each land has a small touch of its own. Butterflies flit over the meadows by day, dragonflies dart in quick straight lines over the swamps, fireflies blink in the deep forest by night, embers drift up off the blasted wastes and their lava, and snow falls gently on the snowfields. All of them are worked out in a shader from where each one hatched, its seed and the time, so the CPU steps nothing; the ones round you are gathered into one mesh and rebuilt as you walk. Heat rises off the lava pools and, more faintly, off the desert's deep sand: upright sheets drawn last over a copy of the picture show it a few pixels off in ripples running up them, never smearing anything that stands in front. The graveyard's trees are gnarled grey trees, with spiderwebs strung in the angles of their limbs and a tiny spider in most webs. Now and then a spider drops down on a thread, hangs there bobbing, then climbs back up (T > Graveyard spiders). The threads are flat ribbons in the web's plane, so a web all but vanishes edge on. Every graveyard has an old iron fence round it, broken into runs with gaps, and a few broken railings across its middle; they belong to the dead and can't be taken down. Frost pines carry snow on the wide foot of each tier. Oaks have a stout trunk flaring into roots and forking into limbs, each ending in a leaf clump, with shaded clumps under the crown and sunlit ones on top; birches are slender white trunks (sometimes two from one foot) marked with black, with thin dark branches and an airy crown of small clusters, now and then already turning gold. Arrows in flight leave a trail of pale after-images that fade (T > Bow: trail). The heat shimmer is a set of pixel offsets the outline pass reads the whole picture by, so the strokes waver with it. Sliders are in T > Ambient life, T > Heat shimmer and T > Graveyard spiders (with spider size).

## Phones and tablets

On a phone or tablet (or with `?touch=1` in the address; `?touch=0` turns it off) the game shows touch controls: a stick at the bottom left walks, and at the bottom right the big attack button swings while held and aims the way you drag it, with cleave, lunge and dash around it (drag one to aim, let go to use it, or just tap). Tap the prompts over doors, loot, tables and stations (and TAP TO JUMP), swipe left or right anywhere on the view to turn the camera, pinch to zoom, and the bar keeps the pack, build mode and the map. The game goes full screen when you tap SET OUT (and again on rising after a death). Probe lighting, lit haze, ground haze, eye adaptation and bloom start off there, and the black point (there to take back the lit haze's washout) starts at 0; they can be turned back on in T.
