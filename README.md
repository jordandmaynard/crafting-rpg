# Emberwild

A fantasy survival game in the spirit of V Rising, with the same low-res, isometric pixel look as [Dungeon Golf](https://github.com/jordandmaynard/dungeon-golf) and [Bone Road](https://github.com/jordandmaynard/board-game-rpg).

You are a human adventurer in a wild land that is generated fresh every time: 1024 × 1024 tiles of terraced ground on a strict grid, with half-tile slopes and ramps between the levels. Fell trees and break rocks for wood and stone, fight what comes out of the wilds, and build a shelter before night falls.

## Play

Serve the folder and open `index.html` in a browser with WebGPU (a current Chrome, Edge or Safari, or Firefox with WebGPU on). The game is in `index.html`; it draws through `src/gfx/`, a small WebGPU library of its own, so it has to be served over http(s) (a `file://` page can't load the modules, and WebGPU needs a secure origin; `localhost` counts). `npx serve` or `python3 -m http.server` in the folder will do. Generating the world takes a couple of seconds.

Every load rolls a new world. To replay one, pin its seed (shown on the title screen and the map): `index.html?seed=12345` or `index.html#seed-12345`.

## Controls

- **Move:** WASD (W always walks up the screen). You always face the cursor as you walk, so you can back away from something while facing it. The forward walk, a backward walk and a sidestep blend smoothly by which way you step against the way you face.
- **Attack:** left click, aimed at the cursor. Hold to keep swinging; the third swing of a combo is a heavy overhead. Hitting trees and rocks gathers wood and stone.
- **Q** whirlwind, **E** lunge, **Space** dash: a forward tumble that you steer with the movement keys, untouchable the whole way, and you come up out of it with a burst of extra run speed for a moment. **Shift** (A on a controller) jumps off a cliff: when you push against the top of a drop with flat ground at its foot, SHIFT TO JUMP shows over your head, and you front-flip down onto that ground. It has no cooldown. The whirlwind and the lunge also cut and break trees and rocks they reach, and every blow gathers wood or stone in proportion to its damage.
- **Tab** opens the inventory (drag it by its top bar, close it with the X or Tab): a paper doll (head, chest, legs, boots, weapon) and a 20-square pack, one item to a square. Click (or right-click) an item to put it on or take it off, or drag it to a square. You start in a cloth shirt, cloth pants and cloth boots with a basic sword. Your weapon gives your attack and your **Q** and **E** abilities: take the sword off and you punch, with nothing on Q or E. Take off the shirt and pants and you're down to heart boxer shorts.
- Wolves (grey and frost) and boars leave a hide where they fall; walk up and press **F** (**A** on a controller) to pick it up. Hides stack to 100 in a pack square. Logs and stone go straight into the pack too, 200 to a square, and there's no separate wood and stone count any more; when the pack is full they fall on the ground as piles (like piles merge, up to 200) to pick up with F / A. One house in every town is the tannery, with a tanning table inside: use it (F / A) to make leather armor (5 hides), leather pants (4), leather boots (2) and a leather helmet (2). Leather gives armor (3, 2, 1 and 1 points, each taking 4% off a blow), shown in the gear window.
- Loot on the ground glows so you can spot it: a beam of light, a pool of light and sparkles drifting up, white for crafting materials and red for health orbs (tune it in T > Loot glow).
- **F** open and close doors.
- **Camera:** hold the right mouse button and drag to turn it freely (the middle button works too). The tilt always stays at 30°. **Z** / **C** turn it a quarter at a time, and the mouse wheel zooms.
- **B** build mode: **1** floors (wood, stone), **2** walls (timber, log palisade, stone, wood fence, fieldstone wall), **3** doors (door, gate), **4** hearth. Each number opens its own row of kinds over the bar, and the numbers then pick from that row. Left click places; drag to lay a rectangle of floor, or a straight wall along whichever way you drag (as many pieces as you can pay for). A right click (without dragging) removes a piece for a full refund. Every button on the bar can be clicked too.
- **O** options (or the OPTIONS button): HDR on or off, and how bright its highlights get. Kept in this browser.
- **M** map: the mouse wheel zooms it (and the minimap) and dragging pans it. It reopens at the zoom you left it at, centred on you. The minimap slides and turns every frame and repaints its contents a few times a second.
- **Region names:** every world names its land afresh. Each patch of a biome is a region (the biggest split into parts of about 15,000 tiles, crumbs folded into their neighbours), named from its biome's own words in one of three patterns: a compound and a noun (Thornwold Forest), the compound alone (the Frostspine), or an adjective and a noun (the Tangled Wilds). That gives over 1,100 names per biome, and no name repeats in a world. Walking into a region for the first time discovers it, and its name goes on the map, tinted by biome, at the point deepest inside it. When that spot would overlap a town or another name, it moves to the next deepest spot that's clear; if none is, it isn't drawn (zoomed out, smaller regions drop off). Lakes, seas, rivers and roads are named too, from their own words (Lake Silvermere, the Swiftwater, Thorngate Trail; over 1,100 names each). You can't walk into them, so coming within 4 tiles of one discovers it, and from then on its name is on the map in blue (water) or tan (roads). Map names are drawn at 16px on a canvas of their own at screen resolution, ringed with a pixel of black.
- **T** tuning panel: the tile height per level (1, the original height, or 0.5; letting go of the slider rebuilds the same world from scratch, so you lose what you've built), the camera (orthographic, or perspective with a field of view you set, and how far in and out it can zoom), the mosaic pixel size and anti-aliasing (the scene is drawn finer and averaged into each mosaic pixel, so thin lines blend instead of breaking up), the hero's light (strength by night and day, reach, colour, and how far toward the camera and how high it hangs, so the side you see stays lit), the day and night (time, clock speed, latitude, season, sun, sky and moon light, haze, exposure), sun and moon shadows (on or off, strength, reach round the view, softness, and how they weaken as the sun or moon gets low: none at 50° above the horizon, full from 60°), the health bars (how long each stays, how long the health just lost waits and how fast it shrinks, the hit flash and glow, sizes, height over the head, opacity and colours), distance haze (where it starts and thickens, how thick, and its colour by day, dusk and night), the tumble (how long it lasts, its speed going in and coming out, steering, the run speed boost after it and how long that fades over, and its cooldown), the walk (cadence, forward and backward stride, arm swing, how wide a sidestep opens, bob, lean), cloud shadows (strength, cover, softness, size, drift speed and direction), the water (clarity, body brightness, wave height and speed, river flow, sun glitter, foam), the lava (ripple height, length and speed, glow, crust, glowing cracks, breathing, drift and flow speed, pattern size, cooled crust at the banks, how many bubbles, how often they burst and how big they are, and spatter), the black edge outline, and a second Sobel crease outline (its own colour, strength, angle and thickness) (how far back the ground behind an edge must be, and how many mosaic pixels thick).
- **G** profiler (or the GPU button), Crafting and Conquest's: the frame rate, the GPU your browser picked, the GPU time of each pass (where the browser has timestamp-query: the world, water, hero and ground haze, Sobel normals, outline and post, shadow map, caustics, lit haze, ground haze sim, GI probes, eye adaptation, bloom) and the CPU time of each part of the frame. Under it, spike capture: turned on, it keeps any frame whose CPU time passes a limit (T > Spike capture), with every call into the renderer and the effects and each part of the game's update timed as a tree you can open down to what took the time, and by default stops the game on it until you press Resume.

### Xbox controller

Press any button on a controller and the game switches to it: the action bar, the door prompt, the help and the map show Xbox button glyphs, and a key or the mouse switches back.

- **Play:** left stick moves, right stick aims (let go and you face the way you walk), **RT** attacks (hold to keep swinging), **X** whirlwind, **Y** lunge (the sword's abilities), **B** dash, **A** opens and closes doors, or jumps down when you're pushing at a cliff edge you can jump from. **RB** opens the gear window, where the D-pad picks a square, **A** puts on or takes off, and **B** or **RB** closes it. Hold **LT** and the right stick turns the camera smoothly instead of aiming.
- **Build (MENU):** the right stick moves a diamond cursor around you. **RT** places (hold it and move the cursor to lay a row or an area), **LT** removes, **X** / **Y** / **A** open the floor, wall and door rows (press again to step to the next kind), **RB** hearth, **B** closes the row or leaves build mode.
- **Both:** **VIEW** map (left stick pans, **RT** / **LT** zoom, **B** closes), **L3** plot outlines, D-pad up and down zoom, left and right turn the camera to the next 45° step.

## The world

- **Terrain:** eight height levels over open water. Each level is one tile high by default (half a tile if you set it in the tuning panel). The lowlands are broad, gentle country. Most of the steep ground is gathered into a few mountain ranges. Terraces are at least three tiles deep (no ground stands more than a level over anything within two tiles of it), so steep ground comes out as a few broad terraces, not a staircase, and the side of every terrace is a slope that reaches half a tile into the lower ground, so you can't walk up it. Cliff faces are rough rock rather than smooth ramps: they bulge into buttresses, are cut by gullies and stepped into ledges, and are banded with strata. The lip wanders and crumbles and the foot spreads out into scree, while the ground on either side keeps its own colour right up to the rock. Only a few rocks of the local kind (mossy stone, sandstone ledges, snow-capped boulders) lie on the faces and at their feet. Banks under water stay smooth. The meadows roll gently and climb mostly by ramps, while the snowy heights are steeper, with sheer terraces and few ramps. Ramps, two tiles long wherever there's room, join the terraces. They are always at least two tiles wide, and always an even number of tiles wide. After the ramps are carved, a flood fill from the first town adds more ramps until every stretch of land you could walk to is reachable.
- **Lakes, rivers and canyons:** about forty lakes fill hollows (ground that lies low for its neighbourhood), each to just under the lowest bank round it, with a sandy shelf round the edge that shelves away into hollows and deeps of varying depth. Two dozen rivers rise at springs on the tops of the heights and wind downhill into a lake (or, if none is in reach, the sea). Their courses come from a search out from the lakes and the sea that only ever climbs, so a river never runs uphill. It is cheapest along the winding lines of a warped noise, so rivers meander, and dear along the brink of a drop, so they go over a cliff head on, as a fall, instead of running along its top. Rivers that meet join. A river lies level across its width and widens as it goes; where it drops a level its water falls in white rapids. Every water surface stays at or under the ground round it, checked again once the land is finished, so water never stands over its banks. Seven deep canyons wind through the high ground, most of them across the way between two towns. Nothing can cross water or a canyon except over a bridge.
- **Biomes:** Bone Road's four (the Whispering Wood, Mirefen Bog, the Hollow Graves, and towns like Emberbrook), plus Goldenreach Meadows, the Sunscorch Waste desert, and the Frostspine snow on the high ground.
- **Waterfall spray:** wherever running water steps down a level, spray and mist rise off the foot of the fall: soft additive puffs lit by the sky, the sun (through its shadow) and the torch, worked out entirely in the vertex shader from each puff's seed and the time, and soft where they meet the water, ground or cliffs (they fade out against the world's depth instead of cutting off). T > Waterfall spray sets amount, puff size, speed and colour.
- **Ore rocks:** copper and iron rocks stand among the rock pillars wherever those grow, about one copper rock to every three pillars and one iron rock to every five. Each is a pillar of the local stone with tapered, four-sided chunks of the metal leaning out of it all round (after Crafting and Conquest's ore), and a couple more broken off at its foot. Hitting one gives copper ore or iron ore, and no stone. T > Resources scales everything you gather or that drops (wood, stone, ore and hides) by 0.5×, 1×, 2× or 4×. T > Ore rocks sets the metals' colours, how many chunks each rock has, and their size.
- **Water apart:** swamp pools and lava never touch water. Each connected body of water and lava runs all one or the other, whichever most of it already is, and swamp pools stay at least a tile away from both.
- **Swamp pools:** the bog's pools are sunk knee deep, with muddy beds and smooth banks sloping down into them. You (and anything else) can wade through them, slowly, and the murky water swallows your legs. Wading makes real waves: a small sheet of water is simulated round you (src/gfx/ripples.js), which your body pushes aside and your legs stir (and so do foes wading near you, except ghosts), and its waves run out, bounce off the banks and die away, their crests drawn like the bank ripples. Their water is still and murky, with its own simpler shader (no waves, glitter or caustics) and its own section in the tuning panel, Swamp water: colour, clarity, body brightness, and the ripples running off the banks (how strong, how fast, how far out), and your wake (how strong, how fast its waves run, how fast they die away, how wide its crest lines are), each with its own colour. Legs stir the water only while walking, so it settles when you stand still, and any swell left standing in a pool sinks away.
- **The Elderwood (deep forest):** the heart of any wood big enough, about ten tiles in from its edge, so it's always ringed by ordinary forest. Its floor is brown-green with fallen needles. Redwoods stand on a loose grid, one to a 4 × 4 block at most. Each trunk is one uneven surface: a foot that splays into a few lopsided buttress roots, which climb the trunk as fading flutes, with streaky bark and a slight wander and twist. They can't be felled, and nobody can claim plots there. Their trunks run off the top of the view. Like walls when you're indoors, every trunk nearer the camera than you snaps down to a stump with a ragged, broken top and dark heartwood inside, so you can still tell it's there, and its shadow still falls whole. The crowns are up out of sight, just above the top of the view: they draw nothing but cast the sun's and moon's shadows. Under them grow ferns, toadstools, logs and the odd young pine. Giant spiders run in twos and threes. Mantraps are rooted: they turn to face you, and once you come within a few tiles they rear back and lunge their heads out to bite.
- **The Blasted Wastes:** burned country like the graves but far more broken, with steeper rise and fall. Its ash-grey ground is strewn with charred pumice and black, burned trees with embers still glowing in their cracks. Smoking vents, holes two tiles across with a lip of ash and yellow sulphur, breathe smoke near you; nothing walks into them and they can't be mined. Craters are punched into it, and it has more bottomless canyons than anywhere else. Lava lies in pools in the crater floors and the hollows, sunk into their banks like small lakes, and lakes and rivers there run with lava too, so none of it can be walked into (a lava river can be bridged). The lava glows by night: a dark crust drifts over the melt, cracked with bright seams, cooling to crust at the banks and carried along a lava river's flow. Slow ripples lift its surface, and bubbles swell up and burst in a flash and a ring of spatter. Monsters keep out of it and roads go round it. Hellhounds (charcoal wolves, horned, with burning eyes and a mane, tail tip and paws of flame) and floating fire spirits haunt it. No towns stand there.
- **Roads:** roads join the towns and three waystones in every biome. They wind round rougher ground instead of running dead straight, and run diagonally as well as straight (a diagonal step is two tiles round a corner, so the road is always joined side to side). They only change level by ramps, cross rivers and canyons on plank bridges (piled into the river bed, or hung from rope rails over a canyon), a street only runs out to the edge of town where it carries on as a road (one that can't get out, past cliffs or water, stops at the last door on it), and every road ends at a town or a waystone.
- **Health bars:** point at a monster (the mouse over it, or on a controller the one you aim at) and a health bar shows over its head, staying 3 seconds after you look away. Its name and a large health bar show at the top centre of the screen too, as in V Rising. That one stays 1 second after you look away, and pointing at another monster replaces it at once. A hit flashes both bars, and the health just lost stays as a pale part that flares white and shrinks away.
- **Towns:** seven per world, each with a plaza, a well, streets and four houses built from the same walls, doors, floors and roofs you build with. Each town is fenced round wherever its edge can be walked across, in wood or dry stone, with a gate standing open where each street runs out into a road. A town no road reaches still gets one gate at the end of a street. Monsters keep out of town, and you wake by the well in Emberbrook if you fall.
- **Foes:** goblins and wolves in the wood, slimes and zombies in the bog, ghosts, zombies and skeletons in the graves, boars and goblins in the meadows, frost wolves and ice wraiths in the snow, scorpions and skeletons in the desert. More come out at night, and they hunt farther.

## Building

The wilds outside town are marked out into plots, as in V Rising: about 450 a world, each some 35 to 50 tiles across. Each one lies on a single terrace and stops at cliffs, water, roads and towns, so nearly every plot is flat ground to build on. A plot may also take in a knoll or dip beside it that is too small to be a plot of its own. Steep or broken ground that no plot fits stays unclaimable. Press **P** (or the map's PLOTS button) to show their outlines on the map and minimap, white until claimed; hovering over one there, or on the ground in build mode, washes the whole plot white. Nothing can be built on a plot until you claim it with a hearth (4 stone, 2 wood): a round stone firepit under a hood, with a chimney that rises clear of any roof built over it. You can lay a floor under it. Build a wall (not a door) on one side of it and it becomes a fireplace built into that wall, with jambs, a mantel and a chimney breast. It stays on that wall while the wall stands. If that wall comes down, it moves to another wall only when just one is left round it, and it turns back into the round pit once there are no walls round it. A claimed plot turns blue. You can have one hearth. Standing near it heals you, you wake beside it when you fall, and taking it down gives the plot up.

Floors need flat ground that no slope reaches into. Walls (not doors) can also run along the top of a one-level cliff: only the ground on top has to be clear and flat, the rocks along that edge make way, and the wall stands on the high side, overhanging the slope a little. Wood floors cost 2 wood, stone floors 2 stone, timber walls 2 wood and 2 stone, log palisade walls 4 wood, stone walls 4 stone, doors 5 wood, wood fences 1 wood, fieldstone walls 2 stone, gates 3 wood, iron fences 2 iron ore, iron gates 3 iron ore, iron doors 6 iron ore. Iron fences are wrought-iron railings with spear-topped bars, iron gates swing the same bars between capped iron posts, and iron doors are banded, studded iron plates in a door frame. Fences, fieldstone walls and gates (wood or iron) stand half height. They block the way like walls and doors, but they never close in a room: no roof goes up, and inside a fenced yard you're still outdoors. A gate opens and shuts like a door (**F**), and neither gates nor fences turn a hearth into a fireplace. Close in a room with walls and doors (any shape, up to about 500 tiles) and a hipped roof goes up over it. Rooms that meet across a wall or door, like a room walled off inside a bigger one, make one building under one roof. The roof hides while you stand anywhere in the building, and every roof hides in build mode. Inside, every wall of the building you're in (your room and any rooms joined to it) that is both lower on the screen than you and between you and the camera, and every outer wall whose inside faces away from the camera, drops to waist height so you can see in. Doors always stand full height. You heal faster under a roof, and monsters can't get through walls or closed doors. They can't strike you through one either (or across a cliff). A monster that has noticed you finds its way round walls, water and cliffs to reach you. If there's no way to you, say you've shut yourself indoors, it loses interest, heads back to where it came from, and pays you no mind for a few seconds unless you hit it. Stone walls are mortared solid, so nothing shows through the joints.

## How it renders

The Dungeon Golf pipeline: a low-resolution buffer scaled up by a whole number, an orthographic camera at a fixed 30° tilt (starting at a 45° turn, where tile edges are a clean 2:1 pixel staircase), and a camera snapped to whole big pixels with the leftover slid in CSS. With the outline on, the frame renders to a target with a depth texture, and a pass on the low-res buffer strokes any pixel whose neighbours within the outline's thickness lie more than the outline distance further back, as Crafting and Conquest's toon strokes do, so strokes are whole mosaic pixels. Scenery is merged into vertex-coloured, flat-shaded meshes, and the hero draws in a second pass so a silhouette shows through anything in front.

The 1024 × 1024 map is split into 32 × 32-tile chunks. Only chunks near the camera are built, nearest first and a few milliseconds' worth per frame, and far ones are let go. Felling a tree or building a wall rebuilds only its chunk.

## Global illumination

Crafting and Conquest's probe lighting, made for a height map instead of voxels (`src/gfx/probes.js`). Every chunk works out a block of a height field, a texel every half tile: the ground with whatever stands on it (rocks, trunks, walls), plus one floating layer above it for tree crowns and roofs, which leaves let some light through and roofs don't. The blocks of the chunks round the camera sit in one texture. Probes stand in columns, one a tile, at four heights over the ground (0.4, 1.4, 3.2 and 7 tiles). The grid follows the camera and is sized to reach as far as the widest zoom shows. Each frame a compute pass traces only the probes inside the current view, as the camera is turned and zoomed, and only those that need it: new to the grid, near ground that changed, or (with bounce light) due a refresh. Rays march through the height field. Lit surfaces blend the probes of the four columns round them, two heights each, and fade out probes that have a cliff or a wall between them and the surface, gradually, so no hard line shows on the ground.

The tuning panel's Global illumination section has the switch, and `L` cycles it:

- **0, off:** the plain sky light, as before.
- **1, sky visibility (the default):** the sky's light, less wherever ground, walls, roofs or leaves hide the sky (under trees, in canyons, indoors).
- **2, bounce light:** all the light arriving, the sky's plus what the ground and things round about give back of the sun or moon (with a shadow ray), the lamps and the torch, and lava's glow. It bounces on through the probes' own light.

There are also sliders for strength, a minimum of the plain light to keep, rays and their reach, light through leaves, bounce strength and rays, and how many probes are relit each frame.

## Lit haze

Lit haze marched per pixel (`src/gfx/haze.js`). The game draws at a low mosaic resolution, so each frame, after the scene is drawn, a compute pass marches a ray through the air for every mosaic pixel. The ray runs from about 14 tiles above you down to just below the ground in view, and stops at what that pixel sees. It takes even steps with no random jitter, so the haze has no grid to show and no dithering, and it never smears across an edge. The haze is thickest on the bare ground (trunks, walls and rocks don't count, so no column of haze stands on a tall trunk) and thins with height, broken into slow wisps that drift with the clouds. It's lit by:

- the sun or moon through its shadow map, so tall trees and buildings throw shafts through it, scattered more forward than back;
- the sky, by how much of it the GI probes see from there (with bounce light, all the light arriving there);
- the lamps and your torch;
- lava's glow: a second compute pass blurs what the ground gives off into a glow map each frame, smoothly within a tile as well as across them, and the glow fades with height.

The outline pass lays the result over the picture. The tuning panel's Lit haze section has the switch and the sliders, including the number of steps. One of them, "Light it adds over things" (lift), scales only the light the haze adds, not how much it hides. Turned down, it keeps shadows and near things dark while far things still fade. It sits on top of the distance haze, which still fades the far ground to the sky's colour.

## Ground haze

Neon Haze's liquid ground haze (`src/gfx/groundhaze.js`): a thin haze lying on the ground in swamps (green), graveyards (white) and the Blasted Wastes (orange). It's a 2D fluid sim in compute over a 48-tile grid that follows the camera, 4 cells a tile. Each frame it advects the air and the haze, eases the air back to a gentle wind (the clouds' way) and the haze back to a patchy resting field that drifts with it, and adds your wake. It also puts back the swirls the grid smears away and solves for pressure so the flow stays incompressible. Trees, rocks and other solid things are walls it flows round. The game fills a site map round the camera every few tiles: the ground's height (the top of water and lava, and draped over cliffs as steep ramps, so the haze covers the rock faces), what's solid, and how much haze rests there and in what colour, by biome (in swamps thickest along the banks and in the Wastes over the lava, thinning away from them; none lies over water), blurred so it fades in at the edges. The haze is drawn after you as stacked slices that follow the ground, lit like everything else (sky and GI probes, the sun or moon through its shadow, the lamps and your torch) and fogged. The haze is read from the sim through a cubic B-spline rather than plain bilinear filtering, and a smoothing step mixes a little of each cell's neighbours into the air's motion each frame (T > Ground haze > Smoothing; the haze itself isn't smoothed, so your wake stays clear), so ripples a cell or two across never build up and the sim's grid doesn't show. The tuning panel's Ground haze section has each biome's amount and colour, and the sim's and the look's sliders.

## Eye adaptation and HDR

The scene is drawn into float targets, so light brighter than white survives until the picture is shown. Each frame a compute pass (`src/gfx/exposure.js`) measures how bright the picture is, haze included: the geometric mean of its mosaic pixels, weighted toward the middle of the screen. The exposure then eases toward what brings that to a target, the way your eyes adjust. It darkens quickly when you step out of the deep forest onto a sunlit plain and opens up slowly again, and by default it never goes brighter than the look as tuned. The exposure stays on the GPU, where the outline pass applies it. The tuning panel's Eye adaptation section shows the brightness it measures and the exposure it's using, and has the target, the limits, the speeds and the middle weighting. It sits on top of the day and night auto exposure.

HDR (the options menu, **O**) switches the canvas to float with extended tone mapping. On an HDR display, highlights (the sun on things, lava, lit haze) then ease off above white toward a peak you set, rather than clipping. Off, or on a display without HDR, everything over white clips as before.

## Bloom and the grade

Bloom (`src/gfx/bloom.js`): each frame, after the haze and the eye adaptation, a compute pass keeps whatever is brighter than a threshold at half the mosaic's size, eased in so nothing pops. Two more passes blur it with a Gaussian, and the outline pass adds it back as a soft glow round the sun's ring, lava, lamps and bright shafts. Last of all comes the grade:

- a black point, taken out of every colour (white stays white), so whatever the haze lifted goes dark again;
- contrast round a pivot brightness;
- saturation.

The tuning panel's Picture section has all of it.

## Crowds

Foes are drawn in crowds, so a horde costs about what one foe does. Each kind's build is made once and cut into pieces: the parts that move together under one joint (the body, or something the pose moves on its own, such as a jaw, a flame, a leg or the mantrap's stalk), merged by material. Each piece is one instanced draw, with a copy for each foe of that kind, so the draw calls stay the same however many foes there are (about 100 pieces across all 14 kinds, plus one draw for every blob shadow). A foe keeps only its joints, which the pose code moves as before, and their places are copied into the pieces before each frame is drawn, farthest first so see-through foes blend in order. When a foe dies, each part of its build flies off on its own as before. Foes push apart only from neighbours on nearby tiles, rather than every foe checking every other.

T > Crowd test keeps extra foes round you (up to 1,000 over the usual 10 by day and 18 by night) and shows how many there are, the draw calls a frame and the frame rate.

## The drawing library (src/gfx)

Emberwild draws with raw WebGPU through a small library of its own, shaped like the parts of three.js the game was first written on so the game code reads the same:

- `math.js`: vectors, colours, quaternions, Euler angles, matrices, planes, rays and view frustums.
- `scene.js`: the scene graph (objects nested in groups, each placed, turned and sized), meshes and instanced meshes (one mesh drawn many times in one call, each copy placed by its own matrix), haze, orthographic and perspective cameras, and the sky, sun and point lights.
- `geometry.js`: vertex buffers and the primitive shapes (boxes, cylinders, cones, the round solids, discs, rings, planes, lathes).
- `materials.js`: lit (standard), unlit (basic) and normal materials, materials with their own WGSL (`ShaderMaterial`: uniforms, textures and extra vertex attributes), textures and render targets.
- `shaders.js`: the WGSL every pipeline shares: the frame and object uniform blocks, haze, flat face normals, cloud shadow, the sun's 17-tap PCF shadow and the lighting (sky light, sun, point lights, Lambert plus a GGX specular lobe), and the built-in materials.
- `probes.js`: probe lighting over a height field (see Global illumination).
- `haze.js`: lit haze, marched per pixel (see Lit haze).
- `exposure.js`: eye adaptation (see Eye adaptation and HDR).
- `bloom.js`: bloom (see Bloom and the grade).
- `groundhaze.js`: the liquid ground haze (see Ground haze).
- `renderer.js`: draws a scene into the canvas or a render target: the sun's shadow map first when it's due, then everything the camera's layers see, solid things nearest first and see-through things farthest first, culled to the view. All of a frame's passes go in one command buffer.

The water, lava, trunk cutaway and outline shaders are WGSL, inline in `index.html`.

## Water

Crafting and Conquest's water surface. Fresnel blends the sky's reflection with the water body (light scattered back up out of it), sun glitter comes off the wave slopes, and whatever lies under the surface shows through the water above it, each colour absorbed at its own rate (red first). A lace of foam runs along the shore, and the water washes it in and out. The waves are scrolling slope noise instead of an FFT ocean. Rivers carry the same water along their flow, using a flow map with two phases half a cycle apart so the stretch never builds up, streaked with foam. The surface is drawn in two passes over what's below: one multiplies it by what the water lets through, then the next adds the water's own light. The sea uses it too, and shelves off from the coast. Because the camera looks down from high above, where Fresnel shows little sky and the sun's glitter is mostly off screen, the water also has touches that read from up there. Slow swells lift the surface itself and lap up and down the banks, sparkles twinkle on them, and caustics play over the bed in the shallows. The caustics are real ones (`src/gfx/caustics.js`, Evan Wallace's method, as in Ultra-Water): each frame a fine grid over one tile of the waves has the sun refracted through the wave slopes onto a bed under it, and each landed triangle adds how much it shrank, so the light the waves gather into bright lines and the dark between them comes from the waves themselves. They are laid on the bed where your line of sight through the water meets it (found from the depth of the world under the water), traced back up toward the sun to where that light came in, so they lie along the slope of the bank and shift with its depth, and they light the bed rather than glow on the surface. The bed under the water is also bent by the waves: the world drawn so far is copied before the water (`renderer.snapshot`), and the water shows the copy a little off where the waves tilt, checking the copy's depth so nothing standing above the water is pulled into it. The swells are smooth shaded. The hero's torch lights the water but doesn't glint on it. Each of these has a slider in the tuning panel's Water section, along with ripple size, how sharply the caustics focus, how deep under the waves they land, and how much the waves bend the bed. There are also sliders for light and shade on the swells, a sheen on their crests, turquoise shallows, and a crisp foam line at the shore with ripples running out from it. Those four are off by default.

## Day, night and clouds

The day and night follow Crafting and Conquest's sky without drawing it: the sun is placed by the hour, latitude, season and north; its light reddens and dims through the air at a low sun (Rayleigh and haze extinction over the air mass); the sky's light shades from night through dusk to day; the moon, running the sun's own arc twelve hours behind (so it climbs as high by night as the sun by day), lights the night; and the exposure opens up as the light fails. Cloud shadows are a tiling noise laid over the world and slowly scrolled, dimming only the sun's or moon's light in every lit material's shader, so lamps and the torch are untouched.

## Phones and tablets

On a phone or tablet (or with `?touch=1` in the address; `?touch=0` turns it off) the game shows touch controls: a stick at the bottom left walks, and at the bottom right the big attack button swings while held and aims the way you drag it, with whirlwind, lunge and dash around it (drag one to aim, let go to use it, or just tap). Tap the prompts over doors, loot and the tanning table (and TAP TO JUMP), swipe left or right anywhere on the view to turn the camera, pinch to zoom, and the bar keeps the pack, build mode and the map. The game goes full screen when you tap SET OUT (and again on rising after a death). Probe lighting, lit haze, ground haze, eye adaptation and bloom start off there, and the black point (there to take back the lit haze's washout) starts at 0; they can be turned back on in T.
